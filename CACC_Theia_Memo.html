Credit Acceptance Corp. | Preliminary Memo for Theia
1. Business Overview

Credit Acceptance Corporation (NASDAQ: CACC) is a Southfield, Michigan-based specialty finance company founded in 1972 by Don Foss — a used car dealer who believed traditional lenders were unfairly excluding credit-challenged Americans. CACC operates a dealer-centric subprime auto finance platform: rather than buying loans outright from dealers, the company advances capital to independent and franchise used-car dealers against future loan cash flows, then shares the residual collections with the dealer once the advance is recovered. This "shared risk" portfolio program creates a genuine alignment of incentives between CACC and its ~10,000 active dealer partners, giving the company a fundamentally different competitive structure than direct subprime lenders like Santander Consumer or Capital One Auto Finance.

The company earns revenue primarily through finance charge income on its $9.1 billion adjusted loan portfolio — 100% U.S.-focused, serving the roughly 55% of American adults with subprime or near-prime credit histories. CACC is led by CEO Vinayak Hegde (appointed Q4 2025), who is layering a digital-first, AI-enabled origination and servicing infrastructure onto a 53-year-old business model. With approximately 2,316 employees and a FactSet-listed market cap of ~$4.8 billion at $447.79/share (March 13, 2026), CACC sits at the intersection of consumer finance resilience and a cyclical recovery from a 2022–2024 credit vintage underperformance cycle. As of February 2026, the company resolved all outstanding regulatory litigation — including both the CFPB and New York AG investigations — removing what had been the primary overhang on the stock for three years.

3. Bull & Bear Cases
Bull Case
Vintage Cycle Bottoming.

The rate of forecast shortfall is narrowing quarter-over-quarter (−$59M in Q3 2025, −$34M in Q4 2025), and the 2025 vintage is already outperforming expectations. As 2022–2024 loans season off the books, EPS has a clear path to $51–77 by 2026–2028E per consensus, implying multi-bagger potential at current prices.

Regulatory Overhang Removed.

Both the CFPB and NY AG investigations resolved simultaneously on February 13, 2026 — Citron Research called this a "once-in-a-decade regulatory reset." Wall Street compliance teams previously restricted analyst coverage due to ESG/reputational concerns; this removal unlocks institutional demand and analyst initiation.

Aggressive Share Buyback Compounds EPS.

CACC retired 61% of its float since 2011 and bought back 12.6% of the company in 2025 alone. With ~10.5M diluted shares remaining, further buybacks compress the denominator and amplify per-share EPS growth dramatically — even at flat earnings, 10% annual buyback = ~11% EPS growth.

Technology Modernization Unlocks Growth.

The new CEO has accelerated the origination system rebuild (70% faster feature deployment), RouteOne/Dealertrack integration, and AI-driven servicing. This removes the friction that drove franchise dealer volume declines, reopening a channel where CACC had been losing share.

Multiple Re-Rating from "Pariah to Platform."

CACC currently trades at a 42% discount to its own historical average P/E. As ESG-driven institutional exclusion reverses and fintech framing takes hold, the stock could re-rate from 9–10x to 14–16x on the same earnings, implying $714–$815 in Citron's base/bull scenario — 60–80% upside from current levels.

Bear Case
Credit Vintage Losses Deeper Than Expected.

CACC has revised forecasted net cash flows downward for nine consecutive quarters. If the 2024 and 2025 vintages continue to underperform — driven by persistent inflation on subprime consumers — provision charges could exceed consensus expectations, suppressing EPS below $40 in FY26 and rendering the current multiple not cheap.

Structural Market Share Erosion.

Subprime auto market share fell from 6.5% to 4.5% between Q3 2024 and Q4 2025. If tighter underwriting (the Q3 2024 scorecard change) is a permanent feature — not a temporary tightening — CACC may operate at structurally lower volumes even as the cycle improves, capping long-term revenue growth.

Leverage at High End of Historical Range.

Total Debt/Equity stands at 417% (FY25), the highest in five years, and interest coverage has compressed to 2.4x from 5.2x (FY23). If capital markets tighten or ABS spreads widen — as nearly happened post-Liberation Day and post-Tricolor bankruptcy — funding costs spike and origination economics deteriorate quickly.

Affordability Crisis Shrinks the Addressable Market.

The subprime share of total auto loan originations has declined for four consecutive years. Elevated used car prices, high tariff-risk on new vehicles, and stretched consumer balance sheets reduce the pool of viable subprime borrowers. CACC management explicitly acknowledged that their consumer is "squeezed out" when affordability deteriorates.

New York AG Litigation Risk Lingers.

While the CFPB withdrew, the NY AG lawsuit continues. If the court denies CACC's pending motion to dismiss, litigation risk resurfaces — potentially reopening the regulatory discount to multiples, triggering additional contingent loss provisions (already $38M in 2025), and chilling new partnerships with ESG-sensitive institutions.

4. Helmer's 7 Powers
1. Scale Economies Moderate

CACC's model has meaningful fixed cost leverage: the proprietary CAPS origination system, servicing infrastructure, and the behavioral database of 5+ million subprime loan outcomes all amortize across growing loan volumes. A larger portfolio means each basis point of spread on each incremental loan generates more net income without proportional cost increases — CapEx was only $1.6M in FY2025 against $2.3B in revenue, a 0.07% intensity ratio almost unmatched in financial services. However, CACC is not a technology platform with pure marginal cost economics; it requires human capital for collections, dealer relationship management, and compliance. The scale moat is real but not as steep as a software or exchange business.

2. Network Effects Weak

CACC does not benefit from classic direct or indirect network effects in the way an exchange or marketplace platform does. Having more dealers does not make the product intrinsically more valuable for each individual dealer, nor does having more borrowers improve the terms offered to the next borrower. The data network effect — where more historical loan performance data improves the predictive accuracy of CACC's proprietary credit model — is the closest analog, and it is real: 53 years of subprime auto loan outcomes in diverse economic cycles is a genuine informational asset. However, the competitive barrier this creates is more accurately categorized under Cornered Resource than Network Effects.

3. Counter-Positioning Strong

CACC's shared-risk Portfolio Program is a fundamentally different product structure than what traditional auto lenders offer, and it is extremely difficult for large bank-based or captive auto finance competitors to replicate. Here is why: major lenders (Ally Financial, Capital One Auto, bank captives) make money by buying loans outright from dealers at a discount to face value. To adopt CACC's model, they would need to (a) stop recognizing immediate loan-purchase gains, (b) build dealer-level residual accounting infrastructure, and (c) accept delayed economics — all of which conflict with their quarterly earnings management and regulatory capital frameworks. In short, CACC does something large incumbents could probably engineer but would never want to, because it would damage how they report revenues and returns. This is textbook counter-positioning. Independent fintech entrants can attempt to copy the structure, but they lack the 10,000-dealer network, the proprietary scoring model trained on decades of subprime data, and the trust that takes 30 years to accumulate — making replication unlikely within a 5-year horizon.

4. Switching Costs Strong

Switching costs in CACC's dealer network are more nuanced than in enterprise software but meaningfully real. A dealer enrolled in the Portfolio Program has (a) trained their sales team on the CAPS origination workflow, (b) built customer relationships that depend on access to CACC's consumer financing, and (c) accumulated "holdback" residuals that pay out over the life of the loan pool — meaning abandoning CACC means forgoing deferred income. The dealer who leaves loses their position in the profit-sharing queue. Additionally, CACC's origination system is now deeply embedded in RouteOne and Dealertrack — the two dominant dealer management systems — meaning switching would require retraining staff to use a different embedded lender portal within a workflow they already use every day. These barriers are not insurmountable (dealers do switch), but they create meaningful inertia that explains why CACC's active dealer count has been remarkably stable even during the volume decline of 2025.

5. Branding Weak

CACC's brand carries limited pricing power with either dealers or end consumers — subprime auto lending is not a premium consumer category. Among dealers, "Credit Acceptance" has strong recognition as a reliable subprime lender with a 53-year track record, but this is better described as trust and reliability than brand equity in the traditional sense. Consumer awareness is low and irrelevant to the business model since consumers access CACC indirectly through dealers, not by seeking out CACC directly. The company's decision to invest in workplace rankings (America's Top 100 Most Loved Workplaces, Great Place to Work) supports talent recruitment but does not translate to revenue-generating brand power.

6. Cornered Resource Strong

CACC's most underappreciated moat is its proprietary dataset: 53 years of subprime auto loan performance data across multiple credit cycles (2001 recession, 2008 financial crisis, 2020 pandemic, 2022–2024 inflation cycle), covering millions of individual borrower outcomes across thousands of dealer contexts. This dataset powers the proprietary scoring algorithm (the "CAPS" model) that determines advance rates — the advance rate is the single most important variable in making the economics work for dealers. No competitor — not Santander, not Capital One, not an AI startup — can acquire this dataset through any means other than time and loans. It took CACC 53 years to accumulate. This data moat is deepened by the fact that CACC's unique profit-sharing structure creates a distinctive loan performance signal that doesn't exist in competitor portfolios. The regulatory license to operate in subprime consumer finance — itself increasingly constrained — adds another layer of resource protection.

7. Process Power Moderate

CACC has developed a distinctive organizational capability around subprime auto collections and dealer relationship management that is difficult to replicate. The company's conservative underwriting culture — maintaining a "large margin of safety in the aggregate in our pricing" even when it means losing volume to less disciplined competitors — is an embedded institutional discipline that has allowed CACC to survive cycles that destroyed competitors (2021 vintage proved this: CACC's 2021 loans are performing well despite industry-wide subprime stress). The remote-first culture, 53rd-anniversary team celebrations, and 30+ year employee tenures all indicate genuine organizational continuity. The new CEO's structured operating rhythms (weekly business reviews, quarterly roadmaps) are a conscious attempt to layer process discipline onto this cultural foundation, which is promising but too new to rate as Strong.

Summary: The strongest moats are Counter-Positioning (incumbents can't copy the shared-risk model without destroying their own economics), Cornered Resource (53-year proprietary dataset and scoring algorithm), and Switching Costs (deeply embedded dealer workflow integration). These three powers compound on each other — the dataset makes the advance rates work, the advance rates attract dealers, and dealer workflow integration creates switching costs that retain them.

5. Porter's 5 Forces
1. Threat of New Entrants Low–Moderate

Entering the subprime dealer finance space requires substantial capital (CACC has $9.1B in loans on balance sheet), state-by-state lending licenses, consumer finance regulatory compliance, and — critically — a proprietary scoring model that can price risk accurately enough to make the dealer advance economics work. A well-funded fintech could theoretically build the technology layer, but cannot acquire 53 years of loan performance data in the near term. The CFPB/regulatory environment (even after the Trump-era relaxation) creates meaningful compliance barriers. The ABS capital market experience and the dealer relationships needed to originate volume represent additional entry barriers. The main realistic threat is large bank-backed lenders (Ally, TD Auto Finance, Capital One) increasing subprime appetite, but their cost of capital constraints and regulatory capital frameworks make deep subprime participation structurally difficult. The effective entry barrier is "moderate-low" in terms of technology but "high" in terms of data and relationships.

2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers Moderate

CACC's primary "suppliers" are capital market investors (ABS buyers, warehouse facility providers, and term loan lenders) and technology vendors. ABS market access is critical — CACC's business model depends on securitizing dealer loan pools, and spread widening in the ABS market directly impacts the cost of funds. The Q3 2025 call revealed that while ABS markets were generally favorable in 2025, spreads briefly froze post-Liberation Day and widened again post-Tricolor Auto bankruptcy. CACC maintains $1.6B in revolving credit facility availability as a buffer, but dependence on capital markets is a structural supplier dependency. However, as a high-volume, long-tenured ABS issuer with a consistent track record, CACC has real pricing power with capital market investors — it is a preferred counterparty for ABS buyers seeking well-seasoned subprime paper. Technology vendor dependencies (RouteOne, Dealertrack, AWS/cloud providers) exist but are not particularly concentrated.

3. Bargaining Power of Buyers (Dealers) Moderate

CACC's primary customers are independent and franchise used-car dealers, of which there are approximately 45,000 in the U.S. The market is highly fragmented — CACC's 10,000 active dealers represent roughly 22% of addressable dealers, and no single dealer or dealer group accounts for a meaningful percentage of CACC's originations. However, dealers have real alternatives: Santander Consumer, CAR Group (DriveTime/Bridgecrest), regional subprime lenders, and increasingly, fintech platforms. The Q3 2024 scorecard change — which lowered advance rates — was immediately punished by dealer attrition, as dealers sent volume to competitors offering higher advances. This demonstrates that dealers do exercise leverage when the economics deteriorate. The introduction of RouteOne integration is explicitly designed to reduce this friction by making switching more painful (workflow disruption), which is an acknowledgment that buyer power is real and needs to be managed through product stickiness rather than unique supply.

4. Threat of Substitutes Moderate

For subprime consumers, the alternative to CACC-financed vehicles is (a) buying used cars with cash (possible for some but eliminates the credit-building benefit), (b) using a credit union's subprime product (limited availability for deep subprime borrowers), or (c) forgoing vehicle purchase (genuinely common, as the shrinkage in the subprime auto market segment confirms). For dealers, the substitute is routing deals to competing subprime lenders. The threat is moderate rather than high because CACC's product genuinely serves consumers and dealer segments that alternatives serve poorly or not at all — a "700+ FICO" lender is not a substitute for a CACC customer with a 520 score and two repos. But the long-run substitution threat is real: if inflation permanently impairs subprime consumer purchasing power, the universe of CACC-eligible borrowers could structurally shrink, substituted by non-consumption.

5. Competitive Rivalry High

Competitive intensity in subprime auto finance is elevated and has remained so despite poor vintage performance across the industry. CACC's market share in its core segment fell from 6.5% (2024) to 4.5% (Q4 2025) — a 30%+ share loss — while competitors continued to originate aggressively. Management has explicitly stated that competition is the primary driver of volume declines alongside the scorecard tightening. The subprime auto space features multiple well-capitalized players (Santander Consumer, DriveTime/Bridgecrest, CAC companies in the credit union space, regional banks) plus a fragmented long tail of smaller lenders. The industry has historically been characterized by winner-takes-all cycles: during competitive expansions, weaker lenders originate aggressively, produce bad vintages, suffer losses, and then pull back — which historically created good vintages for disciplined players like CACC. The current cycle has been unusually long, possibly because large-bank participants have deeper capital buffers that delay the tightening/attrition cycle.

Summary: The competitive environment is mixed but manageable for CACC. The company operates in an attractive structural niche (dealer-finance alignment via profit-sharing), but faces genuine competitive rivalry in the short term, meaningful buyer power from dealers, and macro-driven substitution risk if subprime consumer affordability continues to deteriorate. The moat is durable; the cycle is the variable.

6. What Drives the Stock?

The following 5 variables are what analysts build tracking models around, what Q&A on earnings calls is dominated by, and what would move CACC stock materially in the next 12 months:

1. Forecasted Collection Rate Variance (Vintage Performance). CACC reports a quarterly change in forecasted net cash flows — the "variance" between what was expected vs. actuals plus updated forward curves. Nine consecutive negative quarters have pressured earnings and multiple. A shift to stable or positive variance would be the single biggest positive catalyst — it would signal the vintage cycle has troughed, unlock EPS upside via lower provisions, and justify multiple re-expansion. Bullish: variance turns positive in Q1/Q2 2026. Bearish: declines continue or accelerate driven by 2025-vintage deterioration.

2. Loan Origination Volume (Unit & Dollar). Unit volume was down 9.1% YoY in Q4 2025 (improving from -16.5% in Q3 2025). Volume recovery drives portfolio growth, which drives future finance charge revenue. The RouteOne integration and new CEO's franchise dealer outreach are the primary volume levers. Bullish: YoY volume declines narrow to flat/positive by mid-2026. Bearish: competitive dynamics prevent recovery; market share stabilizes at 4.5% rather than recovering.

3. Share Buyback Activity & Capital Allocation. CACC bought back 12.6% of its float in 2025 alone, making buyback pace a core EPS driver. With ~$2M+ remaining authorization and management signaling "stay the course," the question is whether leverage (currently at the high end of 2–3x target range) constrains further buybacks. Bullish: volume recovery reduces funding needs, leverage drops, and buybacks re-accelerate. Bearish: continued vintage losses consume capital, balance sheet leverage constrains repurchases.

4. Regulatory/Legal Overhang Resolution. The February 2026 simultaneous resolution of CFPB and NY AG investigations was a major catalyst, already partially priced. The remaining question is whether the NY AG motion to dismiss succeeds (pending) and whether other state AGs follow NY's lead in filing state-level claims. Bullish: motion to dismiss granted, no new state-level lawsuits. Bearish: NY AG litigation drags through 2027, other states pile on.

5. Subprime Consumer Affordability / Macro Backdrop. CACC's consumer is deeply affected by used car prices, interest rates, and general consumer financial stress. Management explicitly stated: "anything that impacts affordability for our consumer is a negative for us." Tariffs on auto imports could drive up used car prices (ironically positive for vehicle collateral values but negative for purchase rates). Rate cuts would improve subprime consumer refinancing and purchase capacity. Bullish: Fed rate cuts, used car prices stable/declining, unemployment benign. Bearish: stagflation, tariff-driven vehicle price spikes, rising subprime delinquencies.

The three highest-leverage drivers on total economic profit are:

(1) Forecasted Collection Rate — highest leverage. A 1% change in the aggregate portfolio collection forecast translates directly to ~$90M in net present value change (1% × $9.1B portfolio). A 50bps increase in ABS spreads on $6.4B of funded debt = ~$32M/year in incremental interest expense, directly reducing net income. This is the hidden convexity risk in the model.

The Shared-Risk Model as Structural Differentiator. Across earnings transcripts, sell-side notes, and expert commentary in the AlphaSense corpus, the Portfolio Program's shared-risk architecture emerges as CACC's most durable differentiator. Unlike competitors who buy loans outright, CACC's dealer advance + residual sharing model means dealers have a financial stake in origination quality — they only receive holdback payments when collections exceed the advance threshold. This creates a "natural audit" of dealer behavior: a dealer who originates poor-quality borrowers simply doesn't get paid as well. The competitive significance of this structure is that it allows CACC to serve deeper subprime consumers than any risk-averse direct buyer would touch, while maintaining portfolio quality through incentive alignment. Multiple expert calls in the corpus note that replicating this dealer trust and operational infrastructure — not just the legal structure — would require 10–15 years of investment for a new entrant.

The 2024 Vintage and Scorecard Discipline. The scorecard change implemented in Q3 2024 is a recurring theme in analyst Q&A. The key insight from earnings call analysis is that the underperformance of loans originated before the scorecard change (H1 2024 vintage) was specifically driven by inflation impact on subprime consumers, not by fundamental flaws in CACC's model. The 2025 vintage — originated under the revised scorecard — is described as "performing as expected" or "above expectations" by CFO Jay Martin across multiple quarters. This vintage bifurcation is crucial for the forward earnings thesis: as H1 2024 loans run off the portfolio over 2026–2027, their negative variance contribution declines, improving aggregate portfolio metrics even without incremental volume growth.

New CEO Signal — Technology-First Orientation. Vinayak Hegde's arrival (Q4 2025) represents a deliberate strategic pivot that is underappreciated by the market. Prior CEO Ken Booth was a financial disciplinarian; Hegde comes from consumer technology (e-commerce, digital platforms) and brings an explicit "digital-first, AI-enabled" framework. Key indicators from the Q4 2025 transcript: deal optimization time reduced from 3 minutes to under 2 seconds; AI already deployed in customer service call handling; RouteOne eContracting integration launched in Q4 2025. Expert commentary in the corpus suggests that CACC's prior underinvestment in dealer-facing technology was a competitive disadvantage — particularly with franchise dealers who live in RouteOne/Dealertrack workflows. Hegde is specifically targeting franchise dealer re-engagement, noting that franchise dealers (a) lost volume most sharply, but (b) have slightly better credit performance than independent dealers.

Capital Markets Position and ABS Liquidity. Despite macro uncertainty, CACC's ABS issuance track record provides robust funding flexibility. The company maintained $1.6B of unused revolving credit facility availability as of Q3 2025, and was actively in market with a new ABS deal during the Q3 2025 call at "levels comparable to recent deals." The one structural risk flagged in the corpus is that CACC's ABS structure — which uses a "pooling concept" that contributes uncapped additional loans to existing securitization trusts — means collection rate volatility flows through to trust-level performance monitoring. The 10% early amortization trigger is not currently threatened (trust collection rates running above 100%), but analysts monitoring the 2022 vintage closely, given its cumulative ~8% underperformance, are right to watch this covenants.

Competitive Environment Nuances — "Long-Tenured Competition." The subprime auto competitive cycle has been unusually prolonged. Industry expert calls note that Santander Consumer USA, CAR Group, and regional credit unions with indirect lending programs have not pulled back as aggressively as historical cycles would suggest. One hypothesis in the corpus: larger institutions have post-COVID excess capital that absorbs losses longer, delaying the typical "punish, exit, create opportunity" cycle that has historically benefited CACC's disciplined operators. The implication is that market share recovery may be slower than consensus expects — the typical 12–18 month competitive normalization may extend to 24–36 months.

The Buyback Compounding Engine — A Quantitative Framing. AlphaSense equity research documents repeatedly highlight CACC's share retirement program as a core value driver that consensus models underweight. Since 2011, shares outstanding have fallen from ~27M to ~10.5M — a 61% reduction. At the current buyback pace (~1.3M shares/year in 2025), by 2028, diluted shares could be ~8.5M. Applying the FY28E consensus EPS of $77.11 to 8.5M shares (vs. 11.5M today) implies ~$898M in total earnings — effectively the same absolute net income but distributed over 26% fewer shares. The EPS compounding from buybacks alone is the single most important mechanical driver of the bull case, independent of operating performance.

12. Is This a Theia Investment?

(i) Market Cap Bucket: $4.8B — Mid Cap Acceptable

Mid-cap by Theia's framework ($5–10B bucket, borderline at $4.8B). Well above Theia's ideal sub-$1B microcap sweet spot but below the Large Cap threshold. The market cap is somewhat distorted by share buybacks — CACC has retired 61% of shares since 2011 and the "true" economic size of the business (EV: $11B) is Mid/Large. Not ideal, but not disqualifying — especially given the 4-analyst coverage making it functionally under-followed.

(ii) Analyst Coverage: 4 broker contributors — Under-Covered Strong

Only 4 analysts cover CACC on FactSet — dramatically below the 15+ typical for a $4.8B financial company. This is a direct consequence of the regulatory/ESG overhang that caused institutional compliance departments to restrict analyst coverage. The CFPB/NY AG resolution in February 2026 is a genuine catalyst to unlock analyst initiation (2–4 new coverage initiations likely over the next 12 months), creating a window where Theia can establish a position before the street catches up. For a $4.8B company with $1B+ in annual FCF, 4x analyst coverage is anomalous and represents a structural informational advantage.

(iii) Blockchain / Internet Finance Thesis Alignment: Tangential Tangential

CACC is a traditional specialty finance company with no meaningful blockchain or crypto exposure. The internet finance thesis alignment is tangential at best: CACC is digitizing its origination workflow (RouteOne integration, AI-assisted servicing), which is internet finance in a broad sense, but it is not a fintech disruptor, a DeFi protocol, or a digital bank. The company serves an entirely different risk segment (deep subprime auto) than Theia's typical focus on internet-native financial infrastructure. The new CEO's tech-first orientation adds some alignment, but CACC is fundamentally a secured consumer lending operation. This is the primary weakness of the CACC thesis within Theia's framework.

(iv) Outcome Dispersion: Very Wide — $194 Bear Severe to $1,882 Bull Severe (3-yr) Strong

Few mid-cap companies have as wide an outcome distribution as CACC right now. The bear case ($194, -57%) reflects continued vintage losses and regulatory escalation. The base case ($779, +74%) reflects consensus execution. The bull case ($1,882, +320%) reflects a tech re-rating combined with buyback compounding. The probability-weighted present value using 30% cost of capital is $653 vs. $448 today — approximately 46% implied upside on an expected-value basis, driven almost entirely by asymmetric upside skew from the multiple re-rating and EPS compounding. This wide dispersion is exactly what Theia seeks in a position.
