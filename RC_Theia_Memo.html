Ready Capital Corporation | Preliminary Memo for Theia
1. Business Overview

Ready Capital Corporation (NYSE: RC) is a New York-based specialty finance REIT founded in 2012 and externally managed by Waterfall Asset Management, LLC. The company originates, acquires, finances, and services lower-to-middle-market (LMM) commercial real estate (CRE) loans — primarily bridge loans collateralized by multifamily assets — alongside a growing small business lending (SBL) platform that originates SBA 7(a) loans, USDA OneRD loans, and small-balance commercial mortgages. The company exists to fill the gap left by regulated banks in the $1M–$35M CRE loan market and the $500K–$5M SBA/USDA small business market, where non-bank, fast-turn capital commands a structural premium.

Ready Capital is one of only 16 non-bank SBA 7(a) preferred lenders in the country and was the #4 overall SBA 7(a) lender prior to the 2025 government shutdown. As of December 31, 2025, the company had $7.8B in total assets, a $5.07B CRE loan portfolio (post-repositioning), and $1.2B in SBL assets. CEO and CIO Thomas Capasse co-founded Waterfall Asset Management and has led the company since inception; CFO Andrew Ahlborn and newly appointed CRE Co-President Dominick Scali are leading execution of the balance sheet repositioning plan. The company employs ~300 people. The thesis: RC is in the midst of a painful but self-directed deleveraging that, if successful, will convert a legacy high-leverage CRE lender into a lower-leverage, capital-light SBA/CRE hybrid — but significant execution risk remains against hard 2026 debt maturity deadlines.

Valuation Summary

Ready Capital currently trades at roughly 0.20x book value per share ($1.74 vs. $8.79 BVPS) and 0.57x LTM revenue, representing a 66–70% discount to the peer median P/BV of approximately 0.60x among commercial mortgage REITs. The extreme discount reflects deep investor skepticism about management's ability to execute the $850M+ liquidity plan before the Q3/Q4 2026 debt maturities ($67M in Q3 and $450M in Q4), ongoing CECL reserve builds that compressed book value 14% in Q4 alone, and a 27% non-accrual ratio on the core CRE portfolio as of year-end. The market is also pricing in further book value erosion as the company executes additional NPL sales at discounts. That said, the $8.79 BVPS implies roughly 5x the current stock price, and the SBL platform (30%+ levered yield, top-5 SBA lender) is receiving essentially zero credit — making this a binary special situation: if the 2026 liquidity plan succeeds and the balance sheet is rightsized, the stock is deeply undervalued; if execution stalls, additional dilution or distress is likely.

3. Bull & Bear Cases
Bull Cases

Liquidity Plan Executes on Time. Management targets $850M+ of free cash by end of 2026 via portfolio runoff (36% trailing repayment rate) plus $1.5B of planned loan sales — enough to retire the $517M of 2026 corporate maturities with room to spare. Successful execution de-levers the balance sheet to 2.5x and eliminates the "going concern" overhang entirely.

SBA Platform Re-Rating. The SBL segment generates a 30%+ levered yield and $24.6M/quarter in recurring revenue from only 12% of total assets. As capital allocation shifts from 10% to 20% of the portfolio, SBL earnings could triple by 2027 — and a clean-balance-sheet RC would deserve a meaningfully higher P/BV multiple on an SBA-dominant income stream.

Portland Ritz-Carlton Stabilization. The property (16% of year-end equity) is the single largest wild card. Phase 1 condo sales launched at $737/sqft; hotel RevPAR up 12% YoY; office WAULT of 9.7 years. If the property approaches target economics, it removes a $429M REO overhang and eliminates $0.08/share/quarter of earnings drag.

NPL Sales at Near-Par. The February 2026 bulk sale of 34 loans ($855M UPB) priced in the "high-90s" — far better than feared. If the remaining $1.5B of planned sales execute at similar levels, realized losses will be modest, book value erosion will stop, and CECL reserves reverse, driving earnings recovery in H2 2026.

Rate Environment / CRE Recovery. Floating-rate bridge loans (81% of CRE portfolio) benefit from a higher-for-longer rate environment on existing performing loans. Any sustained recovery in commercial real estate transaction volume in 2026–2027 drives higher origination volumes and gain-on-sale in both CRE and SBL segments, rebuilding the earnings base faster.

Bear Cases

Debt Maturity Crunch. RC faces $67M due in Q3 2026 and $450M due in Q4 2026 — hard deadlines. If loan sale execution slips, portfolio runoff decelerates, or capital markets close, the company may be forced into a distressed refinancing, covenant violations, or equity issuance at current distressed prices, severely diluting existing holders.

Accelerating Book Value Erosion. Q4 2025 CECL reserves increased $150M on an aggressive reserve policy for non-performing loans. With 27% of the core portfolio on non-accrual and $1.4B of sub/non-performing assets still to be resolved, further reserve builds and valuation allowances could drive BVPS below $7.00, compressing the remaining equity cushion.

Portland Ritz Asset Impairment. The Portland mixed-use asset at $429M carry value (16% of equity) is in a structurally challenged market. Office occupancy is only 23%; condo sales velocity is slow (8.3% sold after 2+ years). Any material downward revaluation would immediately pressure BVPS and could require a significant write-down from a non-income-producing asset.

SBA Government Shutdown / Policy Risk. The 2025 government shutdown curtailed $5.3B of industry SBA 7(a) originations and cut RC's Q4 SBL originations 50% to $84M vs. targets. RC's capital-light growth thesis depends entirely on SBA program continuity and scale. Any policy disruption, regulatory change, or loss of preferred lender status would impair the primary strategic pivot.

External Manager / Conflict of Interest. RC is externally managed by Waterfall Asset Management — a structure that creates inherent misalignment between manager fee incentives and shareholder returns. Management fees continue ($4.5M/quarter in Q4 2025) even as shareholders absorb multi-hundred-million dollar losses, and strategy execution may prioritize fee sustainability over speed of balance sheet resolution.

4. Helmer's 7 Powers

1. Scale Economies Moderate
Ready Capital exhibits limited but real scale economies in its SBA lending platform. As a top-5 SBA lender with dedicated origination infrastructure, securitization shelf (fourth SBA CLO planned for Q2 2026), and a proprietary LenderAI underwriting platform acquired via iBusiness Funding, the company can amortize fixed underwriting and servicing costs across a larger loan book. However, the core CRE bridge lending business has relatively high variable costs per loan — each deal requires property diligence, title, legal, and appraisal — so scale benefits are limited in that segment. The benefit is real but not dominant; a competitor with $2B of capital could replicate the infrastructure. The barrier is the regulatory position (only 16 non-bank SBA preferred lenders nationally), not the cost structure per se.

2. Network Effects Weak
Commercial real estate lending and SBA lending are not network-effects businesses. There is no direct mechanism by which adding one more borrower makes the platform more valuable to existing borrowers. There are indirect effects — more historical loan data improves credit model accuracy, and more relationships with brokers and banks improve deal flow — but these are soft advantages, not structural network effects. The LenderAI platform could theoretically develop data network effects over time if it becomes the SBA underwriting standard for bank partners, but this is speculative. Ready Capital is a capital allocator, not a marketplace, so this power does not materially apply.

3. Counter-Positioning Moderate
Ready Capital occupies a genuinely counter-positioned niche relative to large banks. The LMM CRE segment — $1M–$35M commercial real estate bridge loans — is too small for CMBS securitization, too complex for automated underwriting, and too capital-intensive for community banks. Large banks (JPMorgan, Wells Fargo) could in theory offer these products but choose not to, because the unit economics are unfavorable relative to their cost of capital and compliance infrastructure. Similarly, in SBA lending, banks with $100B+ in deposits have little incentive to compete aggressively below $350K — the precise tier where Ready Capital and iBusiness Funding focus. This isn't a pure counter-positioning play (the dynamics are economic inefficiency rather than cannibalization risk), but it creates a durable niche that large institutions structurally under-serve.

4. Switching Costs Weak
Switching costs in commercial lending are low. A borrower who wants a CRE bridge loan in year two will simply seek the best available terms from any lender — there is no technological lock-in, no contractual retention mechanism, and no meaningful data portability friction. Borrowers regularly shop multiple lenders. The SBA lending side has marginally higher stickiness (borrowers build a relationship with a preferred lender who knows their business) but this is relational, not structural. Switching costs do not represent a meaningful competitive advantage for Ready Capital.

5. Branding Weak
Ready Capital has established brand recognition in the non-bank SBA lending community and among commercial real estate brokers in the LMM segment, but it does not possess pricing power or habitual purchasing behavior stemming from brand alone. CRE bridge borrowers choose lenders primarily on price, speed, and certainty of close — not brand loyalty. The "Ready Capital" brand does provide a modest origination advantage with repeat referral partners (mortgage brokers, regional banks), but this is relationship capital, not brand power in the Helmer sense. The brand has arguably been damaged by the 2024–2025 credit cycle performance.

6. Cornered Resource Moderate
Ready Capital possesses two meaningful cornered resources. First, its SBA preferred lender license — only 16 non-bank entities hold this designation nationally — is a regulatory asset that cannot be rapidly replicated; obtaining preferred lender status requires a multi-year track record of SBA loan performance. Second, the iBusiness Funding LenderAI platform, acquired in 2024, represents proprietary technology and bank-partner relationships (it processes SBA loans for third-party banks) that would take years to rebuild. These are genuine barriers, though not impenetrable ones. The SBA license can theoretically be revoked for poor performance, and the technology advantage erodes over time as competitors build similar tools.

7. Process Power Moderate
Waterfall Asset Management's credit culture — built over 20+ years managing distressed and structured credit — gives Ready Capital a genuine process advantage in sourcing, underwriting, and managing LMM CRE loans. CEO Capasse and the Waterfall team have deep expertise in the $1M–$35M bridge loan market, including workout and resolution capabilities that pure-play CRE lenders (or banks) lack. The CRE CLO execution capability (Ready Capital has structured multiple CLOs) and the SBA securitization program (fourth planned for Q2 2026) reflect institutional knowledge that takes years to build. However, the current crisis — 23.4% non-accrual, 14% book value decline in one quarter — also demonstrates that process power can fail under severe macro stress or overextension (e.g., the Broadmark acquisition in 2023 that stretched the platform).

The strongest moats here are the SBA preferred lender license (Cornered Resource) and the CRE/SBA operational expertise (Process Power). The business lacks network effects, strong switching costs, or brand power. Competitive position is adequate in the specialty niche but fragile under stress, as the 2024–2025 credit cycle has demonstrated.

5. Porter's 5 Forces

1. Threat of New Entrants Moderate
In CRE bridge lending, the capital requirements are substantial ($500M+ to build a meaningful platform), warehouse lines require established banking relationships, and building a CLO execution capability takes years. These barriers are real but not prohibitive for well-capitalized asset managers, private credit funds, and insurance companies — all of which have been aggressively expanding into LMM CRE lending since 2022. In SBA lending, the entry barrier is higher: the SBA preferred lender license is nearly impossible to obtain quickly, and the origination infrastructure (broker network, technology stack, servicer relationships) takes 3–5 years to build. On net, threat of new entrants is moderate — meaningful in CRE, lower in SBA — but the existing competitive pool is already large and growing.

2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers Moderate
Ready Capital's primary "suppliers" are its capital providers: warehouse lenders, securitization investors, and corporate unsecured note holders. During normal markets, RC has demonstrated ability to access multiple counterparties (12 warehouse facilities, multiple CLO investors). However, in the current distressed environment, supplier power is elevated: RC had to retire its February 2026 senior note upon maturity (unable to refinance) and faces the concentrated pressure of $517M in Q3/Q4 2026 maturities. Capital markets access is the existential risk for the business model. This force is moderate-to-high in the current period, though it normalizes once the balance sheet is repaired.

3. Bargaining Power of Buyers High
CRE bridge borrowers are highly price-sensitive and have multiple alternatives. The LMM CRE lending market is served by numerous non-bank lenders (Ares, Apollo, KKR Real Estate, Bridge Investment Group), private credit funds, and specialty finance companies. A borrower seeking a $5M multifamily bridge loan can typically obtain competing quotes from five or more lenders within days. Interest rate spreads compress quickly when capital is abundant. In SBA lending, borrower power is somewhat lower (the government guarantee makes lender selection more about service quality and speed than pure price), but still meaningful. High bargaining power of buyers depresses net interest margins and forces the company to compete aggressively on execution speed and relationship management.

4. Threat of Substitutes Moderate
For CRE bridge borrowers, substitutes include conventional bank financing (slower but cheaper), CMBS (only applicable above $10M+), life insurance company loans (fixed-rate, stabilized assets only), and direct equity from sponsors. Each has limitations that create a persistent niche for CRE bridge lenders, but if interest rates decline materially, the relative cost advantage of bridge lending narrows and more borrowers access traditional channels. For SBA borrowers, substitutes include conventional bank term loans, CDFI lending, and equity crowdfunding — all inferior for most small business purposes, but not irrelevant. Threat of substitutes is moderate; the CRE bridge and SBA niches are structural, not cyclical.

5. Competitive Rivalry High
CRE bridge lending competitive intensity is high. The 2021–2022 lending boom attracted enormous private capital into the LMM CRE segment; as the 2023–2025 credit cycle has cleaned out weaker players, the survivors include better-capitalized incumbents (Blackstone Mortgage Trust at $18.9B EV, KKR REFT at $5.5B EV, Apollo CRE Finance at $9.1B EV) who compete directly with RC in the same loan types and size range. RC's significant balance sheet challenges make it a price-taker, not a price-setter, in new originations. In SBA lending, competitive intensity is growing as both banks and fintech lenders (OnDeck, Fundbox) target the small business loan market, though the government guarantee makes SBA loans a differentiated product with more rational pricing. On net, this is a highly competitive industry where RC's current financial distress limits its ability to originate new loans on favorable terms.

The competitive environment is challenging: high rivalry in both CRE and SBA lending, high buyer bargaining power, and elevated supplier power in the current distress. The company's niche is real but not deeply moated. The SBA preferred lender position is the strongest structural advantage and the logical foundation for "RC 2.0" after the repositioning.

6. What Drives the Stock?

Ready Capital is currently a pure distressed-credit special situation. The 4–6 variables that will move the stock materially in the next 12 months are:

1. Liquidity Plan Execution Speed and Pricing. The single most critical near-term driver. Management targets $850M+ of free cash by year-end 2026 to cover the $517M of corporate maturities (Q3 + Q4). Any evidence that loan sales are pricing better than expected (like the February bulk sale in the "high-90s" on carrying value) is strongly bullish; slippage in execution timeline or discount widening is strongly bearish. Analysts track: completed loan sales vs. plan, NPL resolution pace, and Q2 2026 loan sale completion as the key milestones.

2. Book Value per Share Trajectory. BVPS is the primary anchor for valuation of commercial mortgage REITs. Every dollar of BVPS change at a target P/BV of 0.5x translates to $0.50 of stock price. Q4 2025 saw a 14% BVPS decline in one quarter from CECL builds. The market will reward stabilization of BVPS — a single quarter where reserves do not increase would be interpreted as a major positive signal. Analysts monitor: quarterly CECL additions, valuation allowances on held-for-sale loans, and accrued interest write-offs.

3. SBA Origination Volume and Gain-on-Sale. The SBL segment is RC's "quality business" and the most visible evidence of what the company can earn in a normalized state. Q4 2025 SBA originations were only $84M (vs. a $168M+ quarterly target) due to the government shutdown. Recovery to target levels — catalyzed by the Q2 2026 fourth SBA securitization and normalization of SBA program operations — would demonstrate that the capital-light earnings engine is intact. Gain-on-sale at 9–12% premium on SBA/USDA is the highest-quality revenue line.

4. 2026 Debt Maturity Refinancing Progress. $67M due Q3 2026 and $450M due Q4 2026 are existential deadlines. Any announcement of pre-payment, refinancing, or successful debt issuance removes uncertainty and catalyzes stock price recovery. CFO Ahlborn confirmed the company is "sequentially taking out bonds in upcoming weeks and months" — follow-through will be closely watched. Conversely, missed maturities or covenant violations would trigger severe downside.

5. Portland Ritz-Carlton Asset Resolution. The $429M carry value of the Portland mixed-use REO represents 16% of equity and $0.08/share/quarter of negative earnings drag. Any formal sale announcement, accelerated stabilization data, or third-party appraisal above carry value would be a significant positive catalyst. The property's three components (hotel, office, condos) each have distinct stabilization timelines — hotel is the most advanced, condos are gaining momentum, office is lagging.

6. CRE Credit Quality Stabilization. The jump from 4.4% to 23.4% non-accrual in the core portfolio in a single quarter (Q3→Q4 2025) spooked the market. Management insists this was a strategic decision to accelerate resolutions (not organic credit deterioration), but the market needs confirming evidence. Stabilization or improvement in the core portfolio's 60+ DQ rate (currently 6.7%) and non-accrual rate in Q1/Q2 2026 would confirm that the credit cycle is past its worst point.

The three drivers with the most leverage on total revenue are:

(1) CRE Loan Portfolio Size × Net Interest Spread. The CRE portfolio at $5.07B UPB at 7.1% gross yield and 7.2–7.3% all-in debt cost generates thin net interest income in the current period. As the portfolio is repositioned — shrinking from $5B toward the $2B target but with only higher-quality core loans retained — net interest income on a per-dollar basis improves materially. The key lever is: reduce the drag from non-performing, zero-cash-yield assets and concentrate capital in core loans (7.4% gross yield, 5.4% cash yield, 9.4% distributable ROE contribution). Each $500M reduction in non-core/NPL assets at ~1% cash yield, replaced with either cash to retire debt or core loans at 7%+, generates meaningful net interest income improvement.

(2) SBA Origination Volume × Gain-on-Sale Premium. SBA 7(a) is the highest-ROE, most capital-efficient line in the business. At current premiums of 9–10% on 75–85% guaranteed portions, each $100M of SBA originations generates approximately $7–9M of gain-on-sale revenue — with modest incremental capital consumption since the guaranteed portion is immediately sold. With the Q2 2026 SBA securitization opening additional capacity, scaling from $84M/quarter (shutdown-depressed) back to $175M+/quarter adds $6–8M of quarterly gain-on-sale — roughly $0.15/share annually in additional earnings. This is the key forward driver for the "RC 2.0" earnings model.

(3) Operating Cost Structure vs. Revenue Base. Total operating expenses were $59.9M in Q4 2025 — nearly $19M higher than recurring revenue ($41.5M). Management targets 25% cost reduction as the CRE business simplifies. Bringing quarterly OpEx to $45M with only $40M+ of recurring revenue still leaves a deficit, but combined with SBL recovery and debt reduction (cutting $110M/quarter of interest expense as the portfolio shrinks), the math becomes viable. Fixed cost leverage is the bridge mechanism.

The Waterfall Manager Relationship — Structure and Risks

Ready Capital is externally managed by Waterfall Asset Management, LLC under an investment advisory agreement. Waterfall receives management fees ($4.5–5.5M/quarter) and incentive compensation regardless of shareholder earnings performance. This fee structure creates an inherent misalignment: Waterfall's incentive is to grow assets under management (which expands fee revenue), while shareholders need the company to shrink assets aggressively and retire expensive debt. The Broadmark acquisition in early 2023 at $828M — which ultimately contributed to the current CRE portfolio stress — exemplifies this tension. Investors should assess whether "RC 2.0" as a smaller, capital-light SBA lender with less CRE exposure actually serves Waterfall's economic interests or whether AUM growth pressure will reassert itself post-repositioning.

SBA Platform: Acquisition History and LenderAI Technology

Ready Capital has assembled its SBA platform through acquisitions: iBusiness Funding (LenderAI platform, 2022), Fountainhead select assets (2023), Madison One Capital (USDA lending, June 2024), and Funding Circle USA (working capital, July 2024 for $33M). The LenderAI platform is particularly significant — it processes SBA loans not only for RC but also as a technology provider to third-party banks, creating a recurring B2B revenue stream and a data network that improves underwriting accuracy over time. Funding Circle USA, purchased at a significant discount, brought $17M UPB of working capital loans, of which 57% were already 60+ DPD at acquisition — a deliberate distressed-asset buy with a built-in workout. Management's ability to integrate these acquisitions while simultaneously managing CRE workout is a key operational risk.

The UDF IV Acquisition — Strategic Rationale and Outcome

Ready Capital completed the acquisition of United Development Funding IV (UDF IV) in December 2024 for approximately $181M. UDF IV was a non-traded REIT specializing in land development loans in Texas and other Sunbelt markets. The transaction generated a bargain purchase gain of $102.5M in Q1 2025 (management acquired assets at a discount to fair value), partially offsetting the CRE portfolio losses. By Q1 2025, however, the gain had been substantially reversed through subsequent reserve builds. The UDF IV acquisition illustrates both Waterfall's opportunistic credit culture (buying distressed assets cheap) and the execution complexity of integrating a troubled portfolio while simultaneously running a balance sheet repositioning.

Credit Quality: Non-Accrual Surge Explained

The jump from 4.4% to 23.4% non-accrual in the core CRE portfolio in Q4 2025 requires careful interpretation. CFO Ahlborn was explicit: this was not organic credit deterioration but a deliberate classification change. Under the accelerated resolution strategy, the company stopped extending/modifying loans even for good-quality sponsors and instead placed loans on non-accrual as a prelude to sale or forced resolution. The company's CCO Dominick Scali gave a specific example: an institutional sponsor with a Sunbelt portfolio that is "good quality" and "good performance" but was denied extension — the sponsor started marketing the portfolio for sale, and RC expects repayment near par within one quarter. This strategic non-accrual designation inflated the headline non-accrual rate far beyond what underlying credit performance would imply, and is likely to normalize rapidly as loans resolve. Investors who read the raw non-accrual data as credit deterioration may be misinterpreting the data.

Key Long-Term Investor Questions

Three questions will define the long-term investment case: (1) After the repositioning, what is the sustainable earnings power of "RC 2.0" — a $2B CRE portfolio plus a $1.5–2B SBL portfolio with 2.5x leverage? At rough target economics ($120M net interest income + $50M SBL gain-on-sale − $180M annualized OpEx + $30M servicing income), steady-state distributable EPS could reach $0.10–0.20/share annually — enough to support a $1.50–3.00 stock price at a 10–15x multiple. (2) Will Waterfall choose to retain RC as an independent public vehicle or seek a merger/privatization once the balance sheet is clean? Several private credit managers have bought distressed REITs at discounts to book and taken them private — this optionality exists here. (3) Can management stabilize and eventually monetize the Portland Ritz asset near or above carry value? At $429M against $1.4B of equity, this single asset can swing BVPS by $2–3/share and is the most consequential near-term binary.

12. Is This a Theia Investment?

(i) Market Cap Bucket: $283M — Microcap / Small Cap Strong

At $283M market cap, RC sits squarely in Theia's sweet spot (sub-$1B). This is a microcap distressed REIT — exactly the size range where institutional coverage is thin and informed analytical work can generate edge. Note: the 5.75x EV/Market Cap ratio reflects substantial leverage; the equity is a small claim on a much larger asset base.

(ii) Analyst Coverage: 6 Broker Contributors — Under-Covered Strong

Only 6 sell-side analysts cover RC — firmly in the "under-covered" zone (<5 threshold is ideal; 5–15 is moderate). For context, the stock has fallen 66% in one year with essentially no sell-side initiation of new coverage. The buy-side is largely composed of distressed-credit specialists and REIT-focused funds, not the generalist long/short community. This creates an information asymmetry opportunity for deep-dive work on the repositioning plan.

(iii) Blockchain / Internet Finance Thesis Alignment: Tangential Weak

RC's alignment with Theia's internet finance thesis is weak-to-tangential at best. The SBA lending platform (iBusiness Funding, LenderAI) represents a technology-enabled, non-bank lending model that directionally aligns with the digitization of financial services — but this is a small fraction of the current business, and the CRE bridge lending core is a traditional balance-sheet lending model with no internet finance angle. The stock is better characterized as a distressed credit turnaround play than an internet finance thesis name.

(iv) Outcome Dispersion: Wide Strong

The scenario range is exceptionally wide: Case 1 (crisis) implies $1.35 (−22% from today); Case 5 (transformation) implies $7.60 (+337%). The probability-weighted present value at 30% CoC = $3.04 (+75% upside). This is one of the widest outcome dispersions across all Theia memos reviewed — a 5x range between bear and bull — exactly the type of binary special situation where mispricing is most likely. The key risk is timing: the binary resolves by Q4 2026 (debt maturity deadlines) one way or another.

Verdict: NO — Too Speculative, Wrong Thesis

RC is a distressed credit turnaround, not an internet finance thesis name. While the wide outcome dispersion and deep undervaluation are analytically interesting, the investment requires betting on successful execution of a complex balance sheet repositioning within hard maturity deadlines, with an externally managed structure that creates conflicts, in a business (CRE bridge lending) that has minimal alignment with Theia's core internet finance thesis. The SBA platform is genuinely interesting, but it is currently only 12% of assets and could be accessed more cleanly via a pure-play SBA lender. Theia should not invest in RC but should monitor the situation — if the balance sheet repositioning succeeds by Q3 2026 and the company pivots credibly to "RC 2.0" as a SBA-focused lender, the thesis becomes more interesting at that point. Track as a special-situations educational name.

Theia seeks small cap companies that aren't well covered and are strongly aligned on the core internet finance thesis with wide outcome dispersion. RC scores well on size and dispersion, but poorly on thesis alignment and execution certainty.
