Nelnet, Inc. | Preliminary Memo for Theia
1. Business Overview

Nelnet, Inc. (NYSE: NNI) is a diversified financial services and education technology holding company headquartered in Lincoln, Nebraska. Originally founded in 1978 to service federal student loans for two local banks, Nelnet has undergone a dramatic transformation from a company that derived 95% of its pre-tax income from the Federal Family Education Loan Program (FFELP) in 2009 to a diversified enterprise where legacy FFELP represents only 17% of pre-tax income by 2025. The company operates through four reportable segments: Nelnet Financial Services (NFS), which manages a $7.4 billion legacy FFELP loan portfolio alongside a rapidly growing consumer lending and banking operation through Nelnet Bank (a Utah-chartered internet industrial bank with $2.0 billion in loans and $1.76 billion in deposits); Loan Servicing & Systems (NDS, operating as Nelnet Diversified Services), which services $486.2 billion in loans for 13.2 million borrowers under the new Unified Servicing and Data Solution (USDS) contract with the U.S. Department of Education while rapidly expanding into private and consumer loan servicing; Education Technology Services & Payments (NBS, operating as Nelnet Business Services), a high-margin, fee-based platform serving nearly 12,000 K-12 schools through FACTS and over 1,200 higher education institutions through Nelnet Campus Commerce, boasting 97%+ customer retention; and Corporate Activities, which houses venture capital investments (including Hudl, CompanyCam), real estate, solar tax equity syndication, a 27% retained interest in ALLO Communications (a fiber-optic internet provider), and Whitetail Rock Capital Management (an SEC-registered investment advisor).

Nelnet delivered record GAAP net income of $428.5 million ($11.79 per share) in fiscal year 2025, up from $184.0 million ($5.02 per share) in 2024 — a dramatic improvement driven by a $175.0 million pre-tax gain from the partial redemption of its ALLO investment (generating $410.9 million in cash), a $30.2 million gain from CompanyCam, a $32.9 million non-recurring government servicing contract modification, and strong fundamental performance across all operating segments. Excluding non-recurring items, core earnings were approximately $291.2 million ($8.01 per share). The company has reinvested roughly 68% of its historical earnings into new businesses and asset classes, deploying $10.5 billion in capital over the past 12 years across consumer/private loan acquisitions ($2.4 billion), FFELP ($872 million), Nelnet Bank ($278 million), business acquisitions ($264 million), ALLO ($500 million), stock buybacks ($744 million), debt repurchases ($1.6 billion), real estate and ABS ($2.9 billion), and dividends ($365 million). Management describes its capital allocation philosophy as “Berkshire-like” — reinvesting operating cash flows into a diversified portfolio of businesses within its circle of competence: finance, payments, servicing, software, and education. The company’s book value per share has grown at a 16% CAGR from $25 at its December 2003 IPO to over $102 by the end of 2025.

Valuation Summary

Nelnet currently trades at 12.0x LTM P/E and 9.3x LTM EV/EBITDA, well below its 5-year average P/E of 17.8x and 5-year average EV/EBITDA of 15.0x. The stock is near its 52-week high of $142.87, up 36% over the past year — a significant re-rating driven by record FY2025 earnings and investor recognition of the successful diversification away from FFELP. The forward P/E of 15.1x on the sole broker’s FY2026E EPS of $9.20 reflects the market’s expectation that FY2025’s inflated earnings (boosted by $175M ALLO gain and other one-time items) will normalize, but core earnings power of ~$8–$10 per share is sustainable from the diversified business mix. The EV/EBITDA of 9.1x is in line with student loan servicer peers (SLM at 4.4x, Navient at 16.2x) but significantly below SoFi at 31.5x, reflecting the market’s treatment of NNI as a legacy lender rather than a fintech/education technology platform. With only 1 broker covering the stock, the market is almost certainly mispricing Nelnet — the company is effectively an uncovered $5B market cap diversified financial services conglomerate with a 0.95% dividend yield, an 80-cent beta, and a book value per share of $103 (P/BV of 1.3x). The ultra-low coverage creates a classic Theia opportunity: institutional knowledge gap, limited sell-side attention, and a complex multi-segment business that requires deep work to understand.

3. Bull & Bear Cases
Bull Cases

Private Student Lending Boom from Federal Borrowing Caps. The One Big Beautiful Bill enacted in July 2025 instituted new federal borrowing caps for graduate students and parents of undergraduates. This structural shift is expected to accelerate the privatization of the student lending market, dramatically expanding Nelnet’s addressable market for private education loan originations through Nelnet Bank and consumer loan acquisitions through AGM. If private student lending volumes double over the next 3 years (a plausible scenario given the federal retreat), Nelnet is uniquely positioned with its bank charter, servicing infrastructure, and 12,000+ school relationships through FACTS to capture a disproportionate share. This could add $500M+ in new loan originations annually and $25–$50M in incremental net interest income.

Nelnet Bank Scales to Material Contributor. Launched in 2020, Nelnet Bank reached $2.0 billion in loans and $1.76 billion in deposits by year-end 2025, growing at 28% YoY. The bank provides a dedicated funding vehicle for consumer and home improvement loan origination programs, partnering with Edge Focus on innovative ABS transactions ($150M inaugural deal in April 2025, $200M Rule 144A in October 2025, and two $100M+ pass-throughs in March 2026). If the bank doubles assets to $4B+ over the next 3 years while maintaining the current spread, it could contribute $40–$60M in pre-tax income, transforming from a startup division to a core earnings pillar. The bank also enables Nelnet to retain consumer loans on balance sheet rather than selling them, capturing the full economic lifecycle.

Hidden Asset Value Crystallization (ALLO, Hudl, Real Estate). Nelnet retains a 27% voting equity interest in ALLO Communications after the partial redemption that generated $175M in gains. ALLO is a growing fiber-optic internet provider whose value continues to appreciate. Hudl, a sports technology company in which Nelnet holds a significant investment, has expanded aggressively through acquisitions and is shifting toward highly profitable media monetization. The real estate portfolio (multifamily and industrial assets) targets mid- to high-teen annual returns over 3–7 year hold periods with $130M deployed in 2025 alone. These assets are carried at book value on Nelnet’s balance sheet but could be worth substantially more — an ALLO full exit, Hudl IPO, or real estate portfolio sale would surface significant hidden value.

AI-Driven Operational Transformation Across All Segments. Nelnet has aggressively integrated AI across its business: AI-powered virtual agents in consumer call centers, an NBS AI Center of Excellence, enterprise-wide AI coding strategy, Project Horizon (full-launch late 2026) to overhaul Campus Commerce’s billing platform, and AI-based OCR, quality assurance, and network traffic analysis deployed across NDS. These investments are driving all-time high productivity gains in NBS, dramatic improvements in customer service response times, and structurally lower operating costs in NDS following the consolidation to a single servicing platform and 220-person workforce reduction. If AI delivers even modest margin improvement (200–300bps across segments), the EPS uplift on a $2B+ revenue base would be significant.

Fundamentally Misunderstood / Sum-of-Parts Re-Rating. The market still views Nelnet primarily through the lens of its runoff FFELP portfolio, but FFELP represents only 17% of pre-tax income. The education technology business (NBS) alone generates $507M in revenue with 97%+ retention and should arguably be valued at 3–5x revenue ($1.5B–$2.5B) given its SaaS-like characteristics. The servicing business (NDS) earns $509M in fee revenue with improving margins. Nelnet Bank is an early-stage fintech. The corporate venture portfolio includes ALLO, Hudl, and CompanyCam. A sum-of-parts analysis could plausibly yield $180–$250 per share, representing 30–80% upside from the current price. With only 1 analyst covering the stock, this re-rating could be catalyzed by a single high-profile research initiation.

Bear Cases

Structural FFELP Runoff Creates Secular Revenue Headwind. The legacy $7.4 billion FFELP portfolio (down from $11.7B in 2023) is in irreversible decline — new originations were permanently discontinued in 2010, and the portfolio will amortize to zero over the coming decade. FFELP still generates ~17% of pre-tax income through net interest income and floor/derivative income. As the portfolio shrinks by $1–2B annually through repayments, sales, and consolidations, Nelnet faces a secular drag on the P&L that must be offset by growth in other segments. The accelerated prepayment experience from 2021–2024 (driven by federal debt relief programs) has stabilized, but the structural decline is permanent and irreversible.

Government Servicing Contract Concentration and Political Risk. The Department of Education accounted for 68% of NDS segment revenue in 2025. The USDS contract that replaced the legacy servicing agreement in April 2024 features lower per-borrower revenue rates, tiered pricing based on volume rather than borrower status, and performance-based allocation of new volumes. Nelnet also experienced direct volume losses as the Department transferred borrowers to a remote-hosted servicer and as post-CARES Act forbearance borrowers were moved to the Debt Management and Collections System. Any further federal consolidation, budget sequestration, or political decision to reduce Nelnet’s servicing allocation could materially impact fee revenue. The student loan servicing industry is highly politicized, with prominent politicians regularly targeting servicers for scrutiny.

Consumer Lending Credit Risk and Provision Expense Escalation. Nelnet has aggressively expanded into consumer lending through AGM, purchasing over $4 billion in “Pay Later” receivables from Klarna and building a $744M installment receivables balance. This rapid growth in unsecured consumer credit introduces meaningful credit risk that Nelnet did not historically face with government-guaranteed FFELP loans. Provision for loan losses already jumped to $67.8M in 2025 from $54.6M in 2024. In a recession scenario with rising unemployment, delinquency rates on consumer/Pay Later receivables could spike, potentially requiring $100M+ in annual provisions that would wipe out AGM segment profitability. The Edge Focus ABS partnerships, while innovative, also concentrate risk in a relatively new credit strategy.

Interest Rate Sensitivity and NII Compression. Nelnet’s NFS division is fundamentally a spread business: net interest income reached $354.5M in 2025, up from $292.8M in 2024, driven by expanding loan spreads and a shift toward higher-yielding consumer receivables. However, in a falling rate environment, NII could compress as asset yields decline faster than funding costs adjust — particularly for the FFELP portfolio where floor/derivatives provide only partial hedging. The 5.26% WACC reflects the company’s significant leverage (211% D/E), and a rate-driven NII decline of $50–$75M would flow directly to pre-tax income.

Conglomerate Discount and Opacity. Nelnet’s holding company structure — spanning student loan servicing, education technology, consumer banking, solar tax equity, real estate, venture capital, fiber-optic internet, and insurance — makes the company inherently difficult for analysts to model and for generalist investors to understand. The dual-class share structure (Class B held largely by insiders including the Dunlap family at 24.4%) concentrates voting power and limits activist intervention. Management does not provide earnings guidance. With only 1 broker covering the stock and a 57% float, the market’s ability to efficiently price NNI is structurally limited. This complexity discount could persist indefinitely regardless of fundamental improvement.

4. Helmer’s 7 Powers

1. Scale Economies Strong
Nelnet services $486.2 billion in loans for 13.2 million borrowers, making it one of the largest student loan servicers in the United States. This massive scale creates significant unit cost advantages: the fixed costs of maintaining the servicing technology platform, compliance infrastructure, and regulatory reporting capabilities are spread across millions of accounts. Following the 2024 consolidation to a single direct loan servicing platform and the reduction of 220 associates, Nelnet’s per-borrower servicing cost structure has improved materially. Smaller competitors and fintech entrants simply cannot achieve the same per-unit economics at lower volumes, particularly given the USDS contract’s tiered pricing model that rewards larger servicers. In NBS, the FACTS platform serves nearly 12,000 K-12 schools with a largely fixed technology infrastructure — each incremental school adds revenue with minimal marginal cost. Scale is a genuine power here: industry experts confirm that Nelnet’s massive scale allows it to maintain highly aggressive unit economics that smaller, tech-enabled competitors struggle to match.

2. Network Effects Weak
Nelnet’s businesses exhibit limited direct network effects. The loan servicing platform does not become more valuable as more borrowers use it (unlike a marketplace or social network). The education payments platform (NBS) has some indirect network effects: as more schools adopt FACTS, the platform accumulates more data on tuition payment patterns and financial aid workflows, which improves the product. But this is a stretch — schools choose Nelnet for its product quality, integrations, and service, not because other schools are using it. The Edge Focus partnership introduces some network-like dynamics in consumer lending (shared machine-learning credit models improve with data volume), but this is a supplier advantage rather than a true network effect. Overall, network effects are not a meaningful source of competitive advantage for Nelnet.

3. Counter-Positioning Moderate
Nelnet occupies a position that few competitors want to replicate: deep entanglement with the U.S. Department of Education’s student loan infrastructure, complex regulatory compliance expertise, and willingness to operate in a highly politicized industry. Large banks (Wells Fargo, Bank of America) have exited student loan servicing entirely due to political risk and regulatory burden. New fintech entrants focus on private lending (SoFi) or refinancing rather than government servicing. This creates a form of counter-positioning: the regulatory complexity, political scrutiny, and low-margin government contracts that deter competitors are precisely the capabilities that Nelnet has spent decades building. However, this counter-positioning is defensive rather than growth-enabling — it protects existing revenue rather than creating premium pricing or rapid expansion.

4. Switching Costs Strong
Switching costs are Nelnet’s most powerful competitive advantage, particularly in the education technology segment. FACTS has built deep, foundational integrations with major student information systems like Workday and Jenzabar, making its total cost of ownership highly competitive and its product incredibly sticky. Technology directors at partner universities confirm that student billing involves highly complex components — financial aid, work-study, multiple payment plans — and replacing Nelnet’s customized infrastructure is a daunting, multi-year project. The 97%+ customer retention rate across both K-12 (FACTS) and higher education (Campus Commerce) quantifies this stickiness. In loan servicing, the USDS contract itself creates significant switching costs: the Department of Education cannot easily transfer millions of borrowers between servicers, and the technology migration required for NDS’s Velocity platform further entrenches Nelnet’s position. In banking and lending, the ABS and securitization structures create contractual lock-in over multi-year terms.

5. Branding Moderate
Nelnet maintains one of the strongest reputations among loan servicers, providing a distinct advantage in winning massive government and commercial contracts. Former directors emphasize that unlike some competitors who look for loopholes to deny borrower assistance, Nelnet genuinely plays by the rules and helps borrowers navigate complex federal conversion processes. This reputation for being a “good actor” is valuable in winning government contract renewals and school district selections. However, the Nelnet brand also carries a negative perception among some student borrowers who associate it with debt collection, which can occasionally create friction in the campus commerce business where students use the platform for tuition payments. On balance, the brand is a net positive in B2B/B2G contexts but not a pricing power lever.

6. Cornered Resource Moderate
Nelnet controls several quasi-cornered resources: a $7.4 billion FFELP loan portfolio that generates predictable cash flows and cannot be replicated (no new FFELP originations since 2010); a Utah bank charter (Nelnet Bank) that provides a regulatory moat for deposit-taking and lending; deep institutional relationships with the Department of Education spanning decades; and a 27% interest in ALLO Communications, a fiber-optic asset with significant strategic value. The Dunlap family’s concentrated ownership (Mike Dunlap holds 24.4%, Angela Muhleisen 13.0%) ensures management continuity and long-term thinking. However, no single cornered resource is irreplaceable in the way that a proprietary patent or unique dataset would be — competitors with sufficient capital and time could theoretically build comparable infrastructure.

7. Process Power Strong
Nelnet has developed deeply embedded organizational capabilities over four decades of navigating the intersection of government regulation, financial services, and education technology. The company’s ability to successfully transition from 95% FFELP dependence in 2009 to a diversified holding company in 2025 — while maintaining compliance with complex federal and state regulations, managing billions in loan portfolios, and simultaneously building a high-growth education technology platform — demonstrates exceptional process power. The April 2024 USDS contract migration (rebuilding the entire government servicing platform), the June 2024 workforce reduction (220 associates), the November 2025 sale of the solar construction business, and the February 2026 Finastra Canada acquisition all demonstrate a management team that can execute complex operational transitions with discipline. CEO Jeff Noordhoek’s willingness to exit failing businesses (“When it becomes evident that you are riding a dead horse, it is time to dismount”) and CFO Jim Kruger’s meticulous capital deployment tracking ($10.5B over 12 years) reflect institutional discipline that is genuinely hard to replicate.

Summary: Nelnet is a 3–4 power business with genuine strength in Switching Costs (97%+ retention, deep SIS integrations), Scale Economies (13.2M borrowers, 12,000 schools), and Process Power (four decades of regulatory navigation and successful business transformation). Counter-Positioning and Branding are moderate — the political/regulatory moat deters competitors but doesn’t create pricing power. Network Effects are weak. This power profile supports a durable, cash-generative business that compounds through disciplined capital allocation rather than explosive growth.

5. Porter’s 5 Forces

1. Threat of New Entrants Low
Barriers to entry in Nelnet’s core businesses are substantial. Government loan servicing requires federal contracts, years of compliance track record, and the ability to process millions of borrowers across extraordinarily complex repayment plan rules that change with each administration. The USDS contract has a 5-year base period with extension options, creating a structural barrier. In education technology, FACTS’ deep integrations with SIS platforms like Workday and Jenzabar took decades to build and would be prohibitively expensive for a new entrant to replicate. Consumer lending requires a bank charter (or bank partnership), significant capital, and regulatory approvals. The only credible new entrants are adjacent fintechs (SoFi, Flywire, Meadow) attacking specific verticals, not the full breadth of Nelnet’s diversified platform.

2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers Low
Nelnet’s primary “suppliers” are technology infrastructure (internal), capital markets (for securitization and funding), and labor. Technology is developed internally, so there is no external supplier dependency. Capital markets access is broad — Nelnet has executed multiple ABS transactions through Edge Focus and has a revolving credit facility of $435M. Labor in Lincoln, Nebraska is significantly cheaper than coastal tech hubs, and the company’s culture of conservative Midwestern values creates strong employee retention. The February 2026 Finastra Canada acquisition (450+ employees in Mississauga, Ontario) provides geographic diversification for labor. Overall, supplier power is minimal.

3. Bargaining Power of Buyers Moderate
Nelnet’s largest “buyer” is the U.S. Department of Education, which accounted for 68% of NDS revenue. This extreme concentration gives the government significant leverage in contract negotiations, as demonstrated by the lower per-borrower revenue rates in the USDS contract versus the legacy agreement. However, the government’s options are limited — only a handful of companies can service federal student loans at Nelnet’s scale, and the political cost of service disruptions to millions of borrowers constrains the government’s ability to aggressively renegotiate. In NBS, buyer power is dispersed across 12,000+ schools with 97% retention, indicating very low individual buyer leverage. In consumer lending, borrowers are price-sensitive but serviced through long-term contracts, limiting switching behavior.

4. Threat of Substitutes Low
For government loan servicing, there are no substitutes — federal student loans must be serviced by approved entities using approved technology platforms. For education technology, schools could theoretically use spreadsheets, generic payment processors, or build in-house — but the complexity of tuition billing (multiple payment plans, financial aid integration, refund processing, Title IV compliance) makes substitution impractical. FACTS processed over $602M in ACH disbursements through its automated refund solution alone. For consumer lending, alternatives exist (bank loans, credit cards, other fintech lenders) but Nelnet competes on the servicing and origination infrastructure side rather than directly for borrowers. The AI-driven transformation further reduces substitution risk as Nelnet’s platform-level capabilities become increasingly difficult to replicate with manual processes.

5. Competitive Rivalry Moderate
In loan servicing, Nelnet competes with a small number of federal servicers under the USDS umbrella, including MOHELA and EdFinancial, plus private servicers. Competitive dynamics are largely volume-based (new borrower allocations depend on performance metrics) rather than price-based. In education technology, Nelnet faces competition from TouchNet, CASHNet, and newer entrants like Flywire and Meadow. Customers report that Nelnet’s pricing is generally more flexible and cost-effective than TouchNet’s expensive, fully packaged offerings, but newer tech-enabled companies compete on modern UI and API-first architecture. In consumer banking, competition is fierce from established consumer lenders and fintechs. Overall, rivalry is moderate — each segment faces different competitive dynamics, but Nelnet’s diversification provides resilience against any single competitive threat.

Summary: The competitive environment is moderately favorable for Nelnet. Low barriers to entry in core government servicing and education technology, combined with low supplier power and minimal substitution risk, create structural advantages. The key vulnerability is buyer concentration (Department of Education at 68% of NDS revenue), which gives the government meaningful leverage in contract negotiations. Competitive rivalry is manageable due to the fragmented nature of competition across Nelnet’s diversified segments.

6. What Drives the Stock?

The following are the 6 variables that would move NNI’s share price materially in the next 12 months. These are what an analyst covering the name would build tracking models around.

1. FFELP Portfolio Runoff Rate and NII Trajectory. The pace of FFELP amortization is the single most-watched risk metric. The portfolio declined from $8.9B (Q4’24) to $7.9B (Q4’25) to $7.4B at year-end 2025. If prepayment rates re-accelerate (due to new government debt relief programs or FFELP consolidation incentives), the runoff accelerates and NII declines faster than expected — a clear bearish signal. If prepayments remain at stabilized post-August 2024 levels, the gradual runoff is manageable and priced in. Bullish: Prepayments stable, FFELP generates $1B+ cash to redeploy. Bearish: New consolidation wave accelerates runoff to $2B+/year.

2. Nelnet Bank Growth and Consumer Lending Credit Quality. The bank is scaling rapidly ($2.0B in loans, +28% YoY) and the AGM segment is purchasing over $4B in Pay Later receivables from Klarna. Investors will scrutinize: delinquency rates on the consumer loan book, provision expense trajectory ($67.8M in 2025, up from $54.6M in 2024), ABS execution quality with Edge Focus, and whether the bank can maintain deposit growth to fund lending expansion. Bullish: Provisions stable, bank assets hit $3B+, ABS pipeline grows. Bearish: Consumer credit deterioration, provisions exceed $100M, ABS market closes.

3. NBS Education Technology Revenue Growth and Retention. NBS generated record $507M in revenue with 97%+ retention. The market watches for: new school/institution wins (particularly large contracts like the Archdiocese of New York), Project Horizon progress (full launch late 2026), international expansion (Nelnet International in Australia/NZ/APAC), and whether the EANS funding headwind (high-margin Emergency Assistance to Non-Public Schools expired in 2024) is fully absorbed. Bullish: NBS revenue accelerates above 5%, Project Horizon drives upsell. Bearish: Retention dips below 95%, Flywire/Meadow win share.

4. One-Time Gains and Capital Allocation Events. FY2025 earnings were significantly boosted by the ALLO partial redemption ($175M gain), CompanyCam gain ($30.2M), and government contract modification ($32.9M). The market will parse FY2026 to determine whether core earnings (ex-gains) are growing. Future catalysts include: ALLO further monetization (remaining 27% interest), Hudl liquidity events, additional real estate or venture capital gains, and acquisitions (like the $92.9M Finastra Canada deal). Bullish: Additional asset monetization, value-creating acquisitions. Bearish: No gains to offset FFELP decline, dilutive acquisitions.

5. Interest Rate Environment. As a leveraged financial institution ($7.8B in debt, 211% D/E), Nelnet’s NII is sensitive to rate movements. Higher rates generally benefit NII on the loan portfolio (wider spreads between asset yields and funding costs), while lower rates compress margins. The FFELP portfolio has floor/derivative protections that partially hedge downside, but the consumer loan portfolio is more exposed. Fed rate cuts would be negative for NII but potentially positive for loan portfolio values and refinancing activity. Bullish: Rates stable/rising, NII expands.
