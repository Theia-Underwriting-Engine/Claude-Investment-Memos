1. Business Overview

Erebor Bank, National Association is a privately held, branchless, digitally native national bank purpose-built for the "innovation economy" — cryptocurrency and digital-asset companies, artificial intelligence and advanced hardware startups, defense technology contractors, venture funds, and the founders and executives connected to those sectors. Founded in 2025 by Palmer Luckey (founder of defense contractor Anduril Industries) and Joe Lonsdale (co-founder of Palantir and founder of 8VC), with significant backing from Peter Thiel's Founders Fund, Erebor was conceived as the direct answer to the banking void left by the 2023 collapses of Silicon Valley Bank, Silvergate, and Signature — the "debanking" episode that left technology startups, VC funds, and crypto businesses without a primary financial partner that understood their capital structures. The bank received preliminary conditional approval from the OCC on October 15, 2025 (the first de novo national bank charter action under the second Trump administration), FDIC deposit insurance approval in December 2025, and its full national bank charter in February 2026, opening for business on February 8, 2026. Luckey has described the positioning as a "farmers' bank for tech" — a specialized underwriter for advanced technology and hardware sectors the way agricultural banks specialize in farm credit — and the name itself (Tolkien's "Lonely Mountain," the treasure-filled stronghold in The Hobbit) continues the founders' thematic naming convention alongside Anduril and Palantir.

Erebor's business model groups into four product pillars: (1) Deposit products — business operating accounts, treasury concentration, payroll and vendor payment rails for tech firms, funds, trading firms, and select high-net-worth individuals; (2) Commercial credit and collateralized lending — commercial loans for capital-intensive hardware startups, credit facilities collateralized by digital assets, and financing purpose-built for acquiring high-cost AI compute (GPU/chip financing); (3) Stablecoin infrastructure — regulated stablecoin minting, redemption, and 24/7 on-chain settlement under a federal bank charter, including OCC approval to hold de minimis native crypto on balance sheet for gas fees, with deep integration into the Sui Layer-1 blockchain (reportedly facilitating over $1 trillion in cumulative transfers) and an embedded-banking partnership powering Infinite's "Infinite Accounts" dual-rail (fiat + stablecoin) product; and (4) Auxiliary services — treasury management, data processing, and partner-issued corporate cards. Unlike middleware fintechs (Mercury, Brex) that route deposits to sponsor banks, Erebor internalizes the charter, core ledger, payment rails, and digital-asset capabilities in a single vertically integrated entity — the Column / Lead Bank structural model, applied to the crypto/AI/defense vertical. Growth since launch has been explosive: total deposits grew from $1.10B (March 31, 2026) to $4.05B (July 3, 2026) — a near-fourfold increase in three months, on ~400 new institutional and HNW client relationships — and management expects operational profitability by year-end 2026. The bank raised $350M at a $4.35B post-money valuation in December 2025 (led by Lux Capital, with Founders Fund, 8VC, and Haun Ventures), and Bloomberg reported in July 2026 that Erebor is in early-stage discussions to raise at a valuation of at least $8B.

The regulatory posture is the defining structural feature. As a de novo national bank operating at the intersection of banking and on-chain finance, Erebor operates under unusually strict conditions: a mandatory 12% Tier 1 leverage ratio for its first three years (~3x the standard 4% well-capitalized threshold), a capital call agreement obligating investors to inject equity if capitalization falls below triggers, a prohibition on material deviations from its OCC-approved business plan without prior written non-objection, and OCC/FDIC approval requirements for senior executive appointments and any ≥10% change in control during the first three years. These constraints cap near-term ROE and force equity raises alongside deposit growth — but they are also deliberately worn as a product feature: for institutional depositors who watched SVB, Silvergate, and Signature fail, an over-capitalized, federally supervised balance sheet is the selling point. The legislative centerpiece underneath the whole thesis is the GENIUS Act (signed July 18, 2025), which created the first federal framework for dollar-backed stablecoins and catalyzed the wave of bank-charter activity (Circle, BitGo, Ripple, Paxos, Coinbase, Stripe's Bridge) into which Erebor is now selling.

Valuation Summary

Erebor is being marked like a software company but is structurally a bank. At the prospective $8.0B valuation, investors would pay ~8.8x total equity capital raised (~$911M), 2.0x current deposits, roughly 39x Theia's base-case 2028E earnings, and ~4.0x the ~$2.0B of tangible equity the bank will be required to hold by 2028 under its 12% leverage mandate. For context: high-quality public bank franchises trade at 1.5–2.5x TBV and 10–15x earnings; SVB at its 2021 peak — the most generous precedent for an innovation-economy deposit franchise — briefly touched ~3.5x TBV and ~30x forward earnings; SoFi trades near ~3x TBV today; and Circle, the closest "regulated stablecoin infrastructure" public comp, carries a $16.7B market cap on an asset-light issuer model that Erebor's charter emulates but does not replicate. The bull defense of $8B is that Erebor is not a spread bank but a category-defining piece of financial infrastructure — the only de novo full-service national bank charter for the crypto/AI/defense economy, with deposit growth (+268% in three months) that annualizes to a franchise trajectory no public bank can match, plus embedded optionality on stablecoin settlement fees at scale. The bear reading is simpler: the December 2025 round at $4.35B already captured the charter scarcity premium, and the step-up to $8B in seven months prices in flawless execution of a loan book that does not yet exist, at a bank with ~50 employees, one quarter of operating history, and a deposit base concentrated in the most correlated, flightiest sectors in finance. Our probability-weighted 2028 exit value of ~$7.5B (Section 10) discounts back to ~$4.3B at a 25% cost of capital — almost exactly the December 2025 mark, and roughly half the prospective round. The December investors bought the expected value; the July round, if it closes at ≥$8B, is underwriting the bull tail.

3. Bull & Bear Cases
Bull Cases

The SVB Void Is Real, Large, and Still Unfilled. The 2023 failures of SVB, Silvergate, and Signature removed roughly $300B+ of innovation-economy deposit franchise from the market in a single quarter, and no chartered institution has rebuilt it. First Citizens absorbed SVB's book but not its mission; Mercury and Brex are software layers on sponsor banks; Anchorage is a trust bank that cannot lend commercially or take FDIC-insured deposits at scale. Erebor is the only de novo, full-service, FDIC-insured national bank purpose-built for this client base — and the deposit evidence ($1.1B to $4.05B in three months, ~400 new institutional clients) suggests the demand pool is deep. If Erebor sustains even a fraction of this trajectory, a $15–25B deposit franchise by 2028 is achievable, which at SVB-style economics (3.5–4.0% net revenue yield on deposits, 35–40% efficiency) supports $400–700M of earnings power.

The GENIUS Act Makes Erebor the Most-Regulated Stablecoin Rail in America. The GENIUS Act (July 2025) created the first federal stablecoin framework and set off a land-grab for regulated issuance and settlement infrastructure. Erebor's positioning — "the most regulated entity conducting and facilitating stablecoin transactions" — is genuinely differentiated: it can mint, burn, and settle stablecoins 24/7 under a federal bank charter, hold native crypto for gas fees on balance sheet with OCC blessing, and offer clients dual-rail (ACH/wire + on-chain) treasury from a single FDIC-insured account. The Sui integration has reportedly facilitated over $1T in cumulative transfers, and the Infinite Accounts partnership shows the embedded-infrastructure distribution model working. If stablecoins scale from ~$300B to ~$1T by late 2027 as projected, the regulated settlement layer becomes one of the most valuable tolls in finance — and trust-charter competitors cannot pair it with deposits and credit.

Charter Scarcity + First-Mover Window. A full national bank charter is among the scarcest regulatory assets in America — Erebor's was the first new one granted in the current administration, and the application-to-charter path took ~8 months of a uniquely favorable political window. Competitors are getting OCC trust charters (Circle, BitGo, Ripple, Paxos, Coinbase, Bridge), which permit custody but not commercial lending or insured deposit-taking. Replicating Erebor's position requires either years of chartering risk or buying a bank — and the FDIC's change-of-control restrictions make Erebor itself hard to buy for three years, protecting the franchise-building window. Scarcity value alone supported the $4.35B December mark before the bank had meaningful deposits.

The Founder Network Is a Deposit-Gathering Machine. Erebor's cap table (Founders Fund, 8VC, Lux, a16z, Haun, Elad Gil) and its founders' operating companies (Anduril, Palantir-adjacent networks) function as a captive distribution channel: portfolio companies, defense-tech contractors, and crypto firms bank where their investors and heroes tell them to. Anduril itself banked with SVB and lived the collapse. This is the same reflexive dynamic that built SVB's franchise over decades, compressed by network density — and it extends to lending: AI chip financing and defense-tech credit are underwriting problems Erebor's backers understand better than any incumbent bank credit committee. Defense and AI capex are simultaneously in secular boom, giving the niche real tailwind.

Profitability Within Year One + Exit Optionality. Management expects operational profitability by end-2026 — inside 11 months of opening, nearly unheard of for a de novo bank. The branchless model carries structurally high compliance costs but zero physical distribution, so incremental deposits carry very high contribution margins once the fixed regulatory base is covered. Exit paths are multiplying: Kraken is targeting an H2 2026 IPO at ~$20B, Circle's IPO minted a $16.7B public comp, and Capital One paid $5.15B for Brex (Jan 2026) — a software layer without a charter. A profitable, growing, chartered Erebor at 2028 scale would be a headline IPO or a strategic prize for any acquirer wanting an instant regulated on-chain banking franchise.

Bear Cases

SVB Redux — Correlated Deposit Concentration. Erebor's deposit base is deliberately concentrated in tech, crypto, AI, and defense — sectors whose funding cycles, token prices, and sentiment are tightly correlated. This is precisely the structural fragility that killed SVB, Silvergate, and Signature: deposits from a single interconnected ecosystem flee together, at digital speed. Erebor's mitigations (12% leverage ratio, ~50% target loan/deposit ratio, large securities/cash buffer) are real but untested, and the deposit base that grew 4x in three months can shrink just as fast. A 2027 crypto winter or VC funding freeze could simultaneously drain deposits, impair crypto-collateralized loans, and crater AI chip collateral values — the exact triple-correlation regulators worried about when imposing the constraints.

The $8B Mark Prices In Flawless Execution. At ≥$8B, Erebor would be valued at ~8.8x equity capital raised and ~2x deposits — for a bank with one quarter of reported operating history, a $6.0M quarterly net loss, zero loans on the books at Q1, and ~50 employees. Our base case — deposits compounding to $15B by 2028, profitability on schedule, clean credit — still only supports a ~$6.2B exit value at a generous 30x P/E, meaning the prospective round has negative expected return unless the bull case materializes. The December 2025 investors captured the mispricing; July 2026 investors are paying for it. For Theia, entry price is the thesis-killer here, not the business.

Regulatory Dependency Cuts Both Ways. Erebor exists because of a uniquely favorable OCC/FDIC posture and the GENIUS Act; its core products (stablecoin settlement, crypto-collateralized lending, on-chain gas holdings) depend on that interpretive stance persisting. A 2028 administration change, a high-profile crypto-bank failure, or an enforcement wave could restrict products before the bank reaches scale. Senator Warren has already framed the charter as politically connected risk-taking, making Erebor the obvious first target of any backlash. Meanwhile the same regulators cap its economics: the 12% Tier 1 leverage mandate suppresses ROE below ~12% for three years, the business-plan straitjacket prevents opportunistic pivots, and every $1B of growth forces ~$120M of dilutive equity issuance.

The Niche Is Crowding Faster Than Erebor Can Scale. The regulatory clarity that enabled Erebor is enabling everyone else: Circle won final OCC trust approval (July 2026) and brings its ~$73B USDC reserve in-house; BitGo, Ripple, Paxos, Coinbase, and Stripe's Bridge hold conditional trust approvals; Kraken has a Fed master account; JPMorgan's Kinexys processes $5B+ daily and has settled tokenized-Treasury transactions with Mastercard, Ripple, and Ondo; Sony is chartering Connectia Trust; and sponsor banks (Cross River, Lead, Customers) capture embedded fintech flows at volumes Erebor's relationship model won't touch. Mercury ($5.2B mark, pursuing a charter) and Brex-inside-Capital-One attack from the neobank side. The risk isn't losing head-to-head — it's being carved into a well-capitalized, low-velocity niche while each product pillar is unbundled by a specialist.

Unproven Credit in Unprecedented Asset Classes. Erebor's lending differentiation — crypto-collateralized credit and AI chip/GPU financing — has never been underwritten through a full cycle by anyone, let alone a de novo team. GPU collateral depreciates on Moore's-Law time; a Blackwell-generation chip financed at 80% LTV in 2026 may be worth 30 cents on the dollar in 2028. Crypto collateral gaps 40% overnight. Defense-tech borrowers carry lumpy, government-dependent revenue. The bank's credit leadership is new, its models are unproven, and the 2028 loan book we project ($5B+) will season exactly when the first stress arrives. Banks die two ways — deposits or credit — and Erebor has chosen frontier exposure on both sides of the balance sheet.

4. Helmer's 7 Powers

1. Scale Economies Moderate
A branchless bank's cost curve is dominated by fixed regulatory infrastructure — compliance, BSA/AML, cybersecurity, core processing, and capital-markets functions that cost roughly the same at $1B of deposits as at $15B. Erebor's Q1 2026 expense base ($10.6M/quarter, ~$42M annualized) against $4.05B of July deposits already implies rapidly improving unit economics, and each incremental deposit dollar arrives at near-zero marginal servicing cost. The countervailing force is that Erebor is sub-scale against every incumbent it touches: JPMorgan spends more on technology in a week than Erebor's annual budget, and money-center banks amortize their compliance stack over trillion-dollar balance sheets. Within the niche, scale economics work in Erebor's favor — the fixed regulatory cost of serving crypto/defense clients compliantly is a barrier competitors must also pay — but the power is confined to the vertical, not the industry. Rating: Moderate, strengthening with deposit growth.

2. Network Effects Moderate
Two real but early network dynamics exist. First, settlement network effects: as more innovation-economy counterparties bank at Erebor, more payments become instant intra-bank (or on-chain via Erebor's rails) rather than crossing ACH/SWIFT — the same dynamic that made Silvergate's SEN and Signature's Signet briefly indispensable to crypto market structure. The Sui integration (reportedly $1T+ cumulative transfers) and Infinite Accounts embed Erebor as the settlement layer inside other platforms, compounding this. Second, ecosystem referral density: the Founders Fund/8VC/a16z/Lux portfolio network functions as a self-reinforcing client-acquisition graph — each defense-tech or crypto startup that onboards makes Erebor more obviously the default for the next. Neither effect yet has the depth of a mature two-sided network, and SEN/Signet proved settlement networks evaporate when the deposit base flees. Rating: Moderate, with genuine upside if stablecoin settlement volume compounds.

3. Counter-Positioning Strong
Erebor's strongest power. Incumbent banks cannot serve this client base the way Erebor does without damaging their existing franchises: (a) reputational and supervisory legacy — a money-center bank that leaned into crypto-collateralized lending and stablecoin settlement would invite scrutiny across its entire multi-trillion-dollar balance sheet, a risk no incumbent CEO will take for a niche P&L; (b) the post-2023 lesson taught traditional banks to reduce deposit concentration in tech/crypto, not court it — their risk committees are structurally unable to chase this business; (c) sponsor banks (Cross River, Lead) monetize fintech flows but explicitly avoid owning the primary relationship and the concentrated deposit risk; and (d) software-layer fintechs (Mercury, Brex) cannot counter-position against Erebor's charter without spending years acquiring one, during which they remain dependent on the sponsor banks Erebor disintermediates. The bet-the-company commitment to a correlated, politically exposed client base is precisely what incumbents are unwilling to copy — the definition of counter-positioning.

4. Switching Costs Moderate
Primary operating accounts are sticky in normal times — payroll, vendor payments, treasury workflows, and API integrations create real migration friction, and Erebor's model deliberately deepens integration (dual-rail treasury, stablecoin mint/burn tied to the deposit account, embedded accounts inside partner platforms like Infinite). Lending relationships add contractual stickiness, and clients who post crypto or chip collateral cannot easily re-bank mid-facility. But 2023 proved the brutal ceiling on deposit switching costs: SVB's clients — with identical payroll/treasury/API integration — moved $42B in one day. Deposits are the most liquid liability in finance, and Erebor's clients are the most digitally fluent, herd-prone depositors in existence. Switching costs here are meaningful for revenue durability in the base case and nearly worthless in the tail scenario, which is exactly the scenario that matters for a bank. Rating: Moderate.

5. Branding Moderate
In this niche, brand is a deposit-gathering asset, and Erebor's is remarkable for a one-year-old bank: the Luckey/Lonsdale/Thiel halo signals to founders that "this bank is one of us and will not debank you," while the over-capitalized, most-regulated positioning signals to CFOs and fund treasurers that it will not be the next Silvergate. The Tolkien naming and the founders' celebrity generate free, continuous media coverage no marketing budget could buy — the $8B round speculation itself is brand-building. The limits: the brand is polarizing (Warren-wing political attention is the mirror image of the halo), it is founder-dependent (Luckey's attention belongs to Anduril), and it commands no pricing power on the deposit side — innovation-economy treasurers still demand competitive yield. Rating: Moderate — a genuine acquisition asset, not yet a pricing asset.

6. Cornered Resource Strong
The national bank charter is the cornered resource. It is the first and only de novo full-service national charter granted to a crypto/tech-focused institution in this cycle; it permits the specific combination — FDIC-insured deposits + commercial lending + regulated stablecoin settlement + de minimis on-chain crypto holdings — that no trust charter (Anchorage, Circle, BitGo, Ripple, Paxos, Coinbase, Bridge) can replicate; and the path to obtaining one runs through a multi-year, politically contingent OCC process that may already be narrowing. The charter's value is amplified by its timing: Erebor got through the window first, and the FDIC's three-year change-of-control restriction means the asset cannot simply be bought. Secondary cornered resources include the founder network (the Anduril/8VC/Founders Fund deal-flow graph as captive distribution) and the political relationship capital that expedited the charter — though the latter is a wasting asset tied to the current administration. Rating: Strong, with the caveat that regulators who granted the resource can constrain it.

7. Process Power Weak
Process power is accumulated organizational capability, and Erebor — ~50 employees, one quarter of operating history, zero loans seasoned, first stress test unfaced — has not had time to accumulate any. The claimed process advantages (crypto-aware underwriting, from-scratch core banking system enabling 24/7 on-chain settlement, compliance-native onboarding for debanked sectors) are architecture choices, not yet proven processes; the from-scratch core is as much execution risk as advantage until it survives scale. The veteran banking layer (Hagedorn from Valley National/UMB/Wells, CRO Rosenberg, the Atticus Digital technical subsidiary) is credible scaffolding, but SVB had 40 years of accumulated innovation-banking process and still died of a treasury-desk error. If Erebor executes for 3–5 years, its underwriting data in crypto-collateralized and AI-hardware credit could become genuine process power — nobody else will have the loss curves. Today it is aspiration. Rating: Weak.

Summary: Erebor is a 2-power business today — genuine strength in Counter-Positioning (incumbents structurally cannot chase this client base) and Cornered Resource (the only full-service national charter of its kind, in a window that may be closing behind it) — with Moderate and improving Scale Economies, Network Effects, Switching Costs, and Branding, and Weak Process Power. That is a respectable but incomplete moat profile: the two strong powers protect the opportunity, while the moats that protect the P&L through a downturn (switching costs, process power) are exactly the ones still unproven. The 7 Powers lens says Erebor has won the right to build a fortress; it has not yet built one.

5. Porter's 5 Forces

1. Threat of New Entrants Moderate
The full-charter barrier is formidable — a de novo national bank requires ~$276M+ minimum capital (Erebor raised $635M), an 8–18 month politically contingent OCC process, executive teams that clear regulatory non-objection, and three years of constrained operation — and Erebor's own approval consumed scarce political capital that may not be available to the next applicant. But the moat has a gate swinging open: the same OCC has granted conditional trust charters to Circle, BitGo, Ripple, Paxos, Coinbase, and Stripe's Bridge in twelve months, Kraken secured a Fed master account, Sony is chartering Connectia, and Mercury (fresh $5.2B mark) is publicly pursuing charter status. Each entrant attacks a slice of Erebor's stack even if none replicates the whole. The three-year window before Erebor's regulatory training wheels come off is also the window in which the entrant queue clears. Rating: Moderate — high barriers, but an unusually motivated and well-capitalized applicant pool.

2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers High
A bank's true suppliers are its depositors, and Erebor's are among the most powerful in banking: ~400 institutional and HNW relationships supplying $4.05B — an average balance of ~$10M per relationship — means individual clients are material to the liability structure, sophisticated enough to demand competitive yield and covenants, and (as an interconnected community sharing the same group chats, cap tables, and Twitter feeds) capable of acting in concert within hours. Deposit betas in this segment are high and rising as competitors (Mercury at 4%+ yield tiers, treasury MMFs, tokenized T-bill funds) bid for the same balances. Secondary suppliers reinforce the picture: capital suppliers (VCs) hold a capital call agreement with mandatory injection triggers; the Sui Foundation and blockchain networks control rails Erebor builds on; and regulatory "supply" (the charter's permissions) is subject to unilateral repricing by the OCC. Rating: High — this is the single most adverse force.

3. Bargaining Power of Buyers Moderate
On the asset side, Erebor's borrowers — crypto firms, AI hardware startups, defense contractors — have historically had no alternatives (that is the founding thesis), which grants Erebor pricing power on credit: debanked borrowers pay up, and crypto-collateralized or chip-financed credit can carry wide spreads precisely because no incumbent competes. But buyer power rises with every new entrant: a defense-tech borrower in 2027 can shop Erebor against sponsor banks rediscovering the vertical, credit funds (private credit is aggressively entering GPU financing), and eventually Mercury-with-a-charter. Fee-side buyers (stablecoin issuers, payment platforms like Infinite) are large, sophisticated, and multi-homing by design. The structural picture is a temporary seller's market in credit, eroding toward normal competitive pricing as the niche is validated. Rating: Moderate.

4. Threat of Substitutes High
Nearly every Erebor product has a viable substitute for a client willing to unbundle: deposits can sit in tokenized money-market funds, T-bill ETFs, or swept multi-bank networks (higher yield, arguably lower single-name risk); stablecoin settlement can route through Circle directly, through Kinexys for institutional flows, or through trust-charter custodians; credit is substitutable by venture debt funds, private credit (racing into AI infrastructure financing), and equipment lessors; and the integrated "one regulated balance sheet" proposition is substitutable by the middleware stack (Mercury/Brex UX + sponsor bank + Anchorage custody) that already serves tens of thousands of startups adequately. The substitute Erebor really competes against is the status quo assembled stack — and its pitch (one throat to choke, no sponsor-bank risk) is compelling but not compulsory. The rise of yield-bearing stablecoins and tokenized treasuries is a slow-motion substitute for the non-interest-bearing deposit base every bank depends on. Rating: High.

5. Competitive Rivalry High
The regulated digital-asset banking space has gone from empty (2023–24) to crowded (2026) in eight quarters: Anchorage ($4.2B valuation, Tether-backed) in institutional custody/settlement; Circle ($16.7B public, trust charter, $73B USDC) pulling reserve and settlement economics in-house; Kraken (~$20B, IPO-bound, Fed master account) in exchange-adjacent banking; JPMorgan Kinexys ($5B+ daily volume) at the institutional top; Cross River/Lead/Customers underneath in embedded flows; Mercury ($5.2B) and Brex-inside-Capital-One at the startup front door; Column (~$6B, ~$200M revenue) as the infrastructure-bank template; and Old Glory/Varo/Chime/SoFi in adjacent digital retail. Rivalry is intensified by the fact that most rivals are better capitalized than Erebor, several are loss-tolerant strategics, and all perceive the same GENIUS-Act land grab with the same urgency. Erebor's differentiation (full charter + vertical focus) is real but the perimeter is contested on every side. Rating: High.

Summary: Erebor's five-force structure is challenging — materially tougher than the moat narrative suggests: high supplier (depositor) power, high substitute threat, and high rivalry, offset by moderate entry barriers and temporarily favorable buyer dynamics on the credit side. This is the mirror image of a Intapp-style vertical-SaaS fortress: the industry Erebor operates in (banking a correlated, yield-sensitive, politically contested client base) is structurally hard, and the company's edge must come from its two Helmer powers — counter-positioning and the cornered charter — outrunning the industry structure during a 12–24 month first-mover window. The five forces say: the prize is real, the field is brutal, and speed is the strategy.

6. What Drives the Valuation?

Erebor has no stock, but it has a price — set roughly semi-annually by private rounds — and the following are the 6 variables that will move that price materially over the next 12 months. These are what a crossover fund's IC would debate before wiring into the $8B round.

1. Deposit Growth and, Above All, Deposit Retention. The $1.1B → $4.05B surge (March–July 2026) is the entire empirical case for the valuation step-up. The market will watch whether deposits continue compounding toward $6B+ by year-end — and, more importantly, whether they stay through the first crypto drawdown or VC funding air-pocket. A single quarter of net deposit outflow would break the growth narrative and reprice the franchise toward book value; continued 40–60% quarterly growth would validate the $8B+ trajectory. Watch quarterly FFIEC call reports (public for any insured bank) — the only audited scoreboard a private bank can't spin.

2. Whether the ≥$8B Round Actually Closes, and Who Leads It. Bloomberg reported (July 2, 2026) that talks are early-stage and the valuation unsettled. A completed round at $8B+ led by a disciplined crossover investor would set a hard mark and fund the Tier 1 capital that the 12% leverage mandate demands for the next ~$4B of deposit growth. A round that closes flat-to-down, or one filled entirely by existing insiders, would signal that outside diligence found what Section 10 finds — that $8B prices in the bull case. The round's structure (preferred terms, ratchets, capital call amendments) matters as much as the headline number.

3. Hitting Profitability by YE2026 — On Schedule and Clean. Management has publicly committed to operational profitability by end-2026 (from a $6.0M Q1 net loss). Delivering it inside 11 months of opening would be a near-unprecedented de novo achievement and the single best rebuttal to the "story bank" critique; missing it — or achieving it via securities gains rather than core spread income — would hand skeptics the narrative. The composition matters: watch NII growth against the $10.6M/quarter expense base, and whether fee income (stablecoin settlement, treasury) becomes visible as a second revenue line.

4. The Regulatory Weather: OCC Posture, GENIUS Act Implementation, and the 2028 Overhang. Erebor's product perimeter (stablecoin mint/burn, crypto collateral, on-chain gas holdings) exists at the pleasure of current OCC interpretive guidance, and its charter conditions prohibit material business-plan deviation without regulator sign-off. Watch: GENIUS Act rulemaking details (reserve and issuer rules shape Erebor's stablecoin fee pool), any OCC leadership change, Senator Warren-wing oversight letters, and — increasingly as 2028 approaches — election-cycle risk pricing. Any high-profile failure elsewhere in crypto banking would tighten supervision on Erebor first, as the most visible new charter.

5. Loan Book Ramp and First Credit Marks. Erebor had $0 loans at Q1 2026; the equity story requires building a multi-billion-dollar book of crypto-collateralized and AI-hardware credit toward the ~50% target loan/deposit ratio. The next four quarters reveal origination velocity, pricing (are debanked borrowers really paying premium spreads?), and — by 2027 — the first delinquency data in asset classes nobody has underwritten through a cycle. Early credit losses in GPU financing or a collateral liquidation gone wrong would damage the franchise narrative disproportionately, because credit competence is the least-proven part of the team.

6. Competitive Boundary Events. Specific watch items: Circle's trust bank going live with in-house USDC reserve management (does settlement flow bypass banks entirely?); Kraken's H2 2026 IPO (sets the public multiple for regulated crypto-financial franchises — a strong debut lifts Erebor's mark, a broken one caps it); Mercury's charter pursuit (the $5.2B neobank acquiring Erebor's structural advantage); any OCC full-service charter granted to a second crypto-focused de novo (ends the "only one" scarcity story); and JPMorgan Kinexys expanding from institutional settlement into Erebor's mid-market. Each event either validates the category or carves off a pillar.

Erebor's revenue decomposes into four lines with classic bank unit economics, complicated by one binding constraint — the 12% Tier 1 leverage mandate — that couples every dollar of growth to fresh equity:

Key driver quantification (Theia base case, FY2028E):

  • Liquidity book: ~$12.5B avg deposits x 65% held in cash/T-bills/AFS x ~2.3% net spread (asset yield ~4.2% less blended deposit cost ~1.9%) = ~$185M NII
  • Lending book: ~$12.5B avg deposits x ~30% average L/D x ~4.5% net loan spread = ~$165M NII (crypto-collateralized and chip-finance loans priced 300–500bps over the liquidity book)
  • Stablecoin/settlement + auxiliary fees: ~$88M — the least knowable line; at 1bp blended on settlement flow, this implies ~$500B+ of annual monetized volume, vs. the reported $1T+ cumulative Sui transfers to date
  • Total FY2028E net revenue: ~$438M (~3.5% of avg deposits), less ~$170M opex (39% efficiency) = ~$268M pre-tax, ~$206M net income

Leverage analysis: The two highest-leverage drivers are: (1) Deposit growth — each incremental $1B of average deposits at a 3.5% net revenue yield adds ~$35M of revenue, and because the fixed regulatory cost base is already funded, ~$25M of that reaches pre-tax income (~$19M after tax). But the 12% leverage mandate makes deposit growth capital-hungry: the same $1B of footings requires ~$120M of Tier 1 capital, so during the three-year constraint window, growth beyond retained earnings is mechanically dilutive — a ~6x capital-to-earnings ratio on marginal growth that explains why the bank is raising again just seven months after opening. (2) Blended net revenue yield — the swing from a pure liquidity book (~2.3% yield, where Erebor is today) to the mature mix (~3.5%+ with 50% L/D and scaled fees) is worth ~$150M of annual revenue on the 2028 balance sheet, more than the entire projected opex base; every 25bps of yield is ~$31M of revenue and ~1.5 points of ROE. The strategic implication: the business plan lives or dies on converting flighty, expensive-to-hold deposits into seasoned, wide-spread credit relationships faster than the niche crowds — while the capital constraint punishes any growth that outruns profitability.

Regulatory Conservatism as Product Feature. Sacra's analysis — the deepest source in the record — reframes Erebor's constraints as strategy: the 12% Tier 1 leverage mandate, the highly liquid balance sheet, and the prohibition on business-plan deviation are not just imposed costs but a sales pitch to "risk-sensitive institutional depositors who previously witnessed the rapid collapse of prior tech-focused banks." The bank deliberately runs a ~50% target loan/deposit ratio (half industry norm) and wears its over-capitalization as marketing. The July deposit data ($4.05B, +268% in three months) suggests the pitch is landing. The corollary insight: Erebor's expense profile is inverted relative to a normal de novo — it saves on branches and tellers but carries "exceptionally high compliance, cybersecurity, legal, and technical-governance costs," meaning the fixed cost base is regulatory, not physical, and operating leverage arrives only with deposit scale.

The Deposit Concentration Warning Is Loud and Specific. Both reports independently flag the same structural fragility in nearly identical language: Erebor's customer base is concentrated in "interconnected tech/crypto ecosystems" that "could trigger correlated deposit flight during industry downturns," explicitly mirroring "Silicon Valley Bank's failure pattern" and the Silvergate/Signature precedents. This is the consensus bear case — notable because it is the only risk on which the fragmented source record fully agrees. The reports also surface the political dimension: Senator Warren has characterized the charter approval as "a politically connected and risky venture that could pose a risk to taxpayers," establishing Erebor as the first target of any supervisory backlash. An underwriter should treat the SVB-pattern risk as base-rate-relevant, not merely narrative: three banks with this deposit profile failed within one quarter in 2023.

Market Misunderstanding: Valuation Timing and the Wrong Comp Set. The business-analysis report makes an underappreciated point about the marks: "historical valuation assessments occurred before the bank received its charter or launched operations" — the $4.35B December 2025 round priced a pre-operational shell with a conditional approval, meaning the market has never actually priced Erebor as an operating bank; the ≥$8B discussion is the first attempt, on one quarter of data. Separately, the same report benchmarks Erebor against Varo ($374M assets), Chime ($2.2B revenue), and SoFi ($51B assets) — a framing Theia regards as a category error: those are mass-market retail neobanks, while Erebor's economics (relationship-dense, ~$10M average balances, B2B credit) make Column, Mercury, Anchorage, and pre-2022 SVB the correct references. The prevalence of the wrong comp set in the public record is itself useful: it suggests the market has not yet converged on how to value this franchise, which is where dispersion — and mispricing — lives.

Key Investor Questions. Based on the gaps in the AlphaSense record, the questions that would most sharpen conviction: (1) What is the deposit composition — how much is operating/payments cash (sticky) vs. yield-seeking treasury (flighty), and what share is from crypto-native firms vs. defense/AI? (3) What is monetized stablecoin settlement volume and the realized fee rate — is the reported $1T+ Sui transfer figure revenue-bearing or vanity flow? (4) What does the loan pipeline look like — origination commitments, pricing spreads, and collateral haircuts on crypto and GPU-backed facilities? (5) At what valuation and structure does the July round actually close, and does a new outside lead price it — or is it an insider mark?
