Carvana Co | Preliminary Memo for Theia
1. Business Overview

Carvana Co (CVNA) is the largest purely digital used-car retailer in the United States, operating an asset-light, vertically integrated platform that allows consumers to buy, sell, and finance a used vehicle entirely online — with home delivery or vending-machine pickup — without ever stepping into a traditional dealership. Founded in 2012 and headquartered in Tempe, Arizona, Carvana is led by CEO Ernie Garcia III and employs approximately 16,000 people. The company went public in April 2017 and reported 416,348 retail units sold in FY2025, making it the second-largest used-car retailer in the US behind CarMax.

Carvana's revenue model has three interlocking components: (1) Retail Vehicle Sales — the spread between acquisition cost and retail selling price (~71.5% of revenue in FY25); (2) Wholesale Vehicle Sales — vehicles that don't meet retail standards auctioned to dealers via ADESA, the wholesale auction network Carvana acquired in 2022 (~19.9%); and (3) Finance & Insurance / Other Revenue — ancillary revenue from vehicle financing, GAP, and service contracts (~8.5%). The key unit-level profit metric is GPU (Gross Profit per Unit), which reached $7,044 in FY2025 and is management's primary KPI. The long-term strategic vision is to scale to 3 million+ annual retail units — roughly 7–8x current volume — by leveraging the fixed-cost base of 33 Inspection & Reconditioning Centers (IRCs) that can collectively handle 4M+ units/year with minimal incremental capital, generating a step-function improvement in operating leverage.

Carvana nearly went bankrupt in 2022–2023 as rising interest rates crushed used-car demand, GPU collapsed, and the $2.2B ADESA acquisition at the top of the market left the balance sheet severely overleveraged. The company executed a landmark debt restructuring in late 2023, eliminating ~$1.3B of principal and extending maturities, and subsequently delivered a dramatic operational turnaround: FY2025 EBITDA of $2.16B (10.6% margin) versus effectively zero in FY2023. This recovery — from near-insolvency to large-cap status in 24 months — is the defining context for the current investment debate. At $300.15, CVNA trades at 24.1x FY26E EV/EBITDA, pricing in continued execution toward the 3M-unit vision.

Valuation Summary

Carvana currently trades at 24.1x FY26E EV/EBITDA and 40.6x FY26E P/E, representing a 76% and 33% premium, respectively, to the eCommerce peer median of 13.7x EV/EBITDA and 30.6x P/E — a significant premium that reflects the market's confidence in Carvana's unit economics flywheel and the exceptional operating leverage embedded in its fixed IRC cost base. The current elevated multiple is driven primarily by the magnitude and speed of the FY2023–25 margin recovery, which surprised virtually every sell-side model, and by management's credible long-term guide toward 3M annual units — a scenario that would put FY2028+ EBITDA margins in the 13–15%+ range versus today's 10.6%. Bears point to the $4.5B in consolidated debt, the auto macro sensitivity of both vehicle volumes and F&I income, and competition from CarMax's ACO (digital-first) initiative as risks that the current multiple does not adequately discount. At current prices, the stock is clearly pricing in continued flawless execution: any deceleration in unit growth, GPU compression from used-car price normalization, or interest rate shock to F&I economics would disproportionately reprice the multiple. Given the company's 19.8% gross margin — competitive but not dominant relative to the platform peer set — and the rapidly improving but still early-stage balance sheet health, the valuation appears fair-to-stretched relative to available upside under a base-case scenario, with the bull case requiring genuine market share displacement at scale.

3. Bull & Bear Cases
Bull Cases
IRC Operating Leverage Inflection.

Carvana's 33 IRCs have theoretical capacity of 4M+ annual units versus current throughput of ~416K. Every incremental unit costs dramatically less to recondition than the marginal cost of building new capacity — as volume scales to 600K–800K units and beyond, fixed IRC overhead amortizes sharply, driving EBITDA margins from ~10.6% toward 15%+ with zero incremental capital expenditure on reconditioning capacity.

F&I Monetization Remains Structurally Elevated.

Finance & Insurance is the highest-margin revenue line, contributing ~$2,651 of the $7,044 total GPU in FY2025. If rates stabilize or decline, loss rates on originated loans normalize, and Carvana's finance penetration rate holds above 60%, F&I GPU could expand toward $3,000+, driving disproportionate profit growth without any unit volume assumptions.

Market Share in a Fragmented, Massive TAM.

The US used-car market transacts ~40 million units annually. Carvana at 416K units holds roughly 1% share. Even reaching 1.5–2% share would double or triple unit volumes. The fragmentation of the dealer landscape (many independent dealers with no digital capability) and the secular shift toward online car-buying create a durable multi-year tailwind for Carvana's value proposition.

Balance Sheet Normalization Creates Financial Flexibility.

With Debt/Equity declining from 2,794% in FY23 to a projected 38% by FY27, Carvana will exit its financial distress overhang and gain access to investment-grade capital markets. This enables cheaper financing for vehicle inventory (lower floorplan costs), potentially lower WACC as the credit risk premium fades, and the ability to be more aggressive on vehicle acquisition — the raw material of the business.

ADESA Wholesale Network as Standalone Profit Engine.

The ADESA acquisition — widely criticized as a capital-destroying mistake in 2022 — is now becoming a strategic asset. CVNA uses ADESA to wholesale non-retail-grade vehicles at economics that no standalone used-car dealer can match, and ADESA provides proprietary insight into wholesale pricing that improves retail acquisition accuracy. As volumes scale, ADESA's fixed auction infrastructure generates incremental profit with near-zero marginal cost.

Bear Cases
Used-Car Price Deflation Compresses Retail GPU.

Carvana's retail GPU is partly a function of used-car prices: when prices rise, vehicles appreciating in inventory generate windfall gains; when prices normalize or fall, the opposite occurs. A structural decline in used-car prices — driven by improving new-car supply, easing pandemic-era shortages, or rising lease returns — could compress retail GPU from $3,421 toward $2,500 or below, materially reducing EBITDA per unit and total profitability regardless of volume growth.

F&I Income Rate Sensitivity.

A large portion of Carvana's F&I GPU comes from the spread between vehicle loan origination rates and the rates at which loans are sold into the securitization market. If benchmark rates rise or credit spreads widen during a risk-off episode, this spread compresses sharply — as it did in 2022 — potentially turning F&I from a $2,651 GPU contributor to a marginal or negative one. This is the most binary risk in the model and is opaque to outside analysts.

CarMax ACO Response Intensifies Competition.

CarMax has invested heavily in its omni-channel digital offering (ACO — Auto Commerce Online), enabling customers to transact online and via physical stores with a unified experience. With 3x Carvana's unit volume, CarMax can match pricing, improve digital convenience, and respond to CVNA's core value proposition. Competition from CarMax, Autonation Digital, and private-equity-backed roll-ups could prevent Carvana from reaching the 2%+ market share implied by the bull case.

Macro Deterioration Hits Both Volume and Credit.

Used-car demand is cyclical and credit-sensitive. In a recession scenario, both purchase volumes and F&I loss rates move adversely simultaneously — the worst possible combination for Carvana's unit economics. Carvana originates ~$4–5B of vehicle loans annually; even a moderate rise in default rates requires holding more capital against residual risk retained in securitizations, constraining both cash flow and financing flexibility.

Debt Overhang and Refinancing Risk Through 2028.

Despite the 2023 restructuring, Carvana still carries $4.45B in consolidated debt at a blended rate well above investment grade. A significant portion matures or faces repricing risk in the 2025–2028 window. If the macro or company-specific environment deteriorates before CVNA reaches investment-grade metrics, refinancing could be expensive or dilutive. The stock has already proven it can lose 80%+ in value within a single year; the leverage structure guarantees outsized equity volatility in any downside scenario.

4. Helmer's 7 Powers
1. Scale Economies Strong

Carvana's cost structure is dominated by fixed and semi-fixed costs — the IRC network, proprietary logistics infrastructure (last-mile delivery vehicles, vending machines), technology platform maintenance, and corporate overhead — that do not scale linearly with unit volume. At 416K units in FY2025, management estimates the IRC network operates at roughly 10% of theoretical capacity. As units scale toward 1M, 2M, and eventually 3M, these fixed costs amortize over a dramatically larger base: the same IRC in a given city handles 30K units/year as efficiently as it handles 10K units/year, with effectively zero marginal cost increase. This is the central thesis for EBITDA margin expansion from 10.6% today toward 15%+ at scale. The barrier to challengers is enormous: replicating 33 IRCs and a nationwide logistics network requires billions in capital and years of real estate permitting, construction, and equipment installation that no startup can shortcut. Even CarMax, with significant physical infrastructure, lacks Carvana's purely digital acquisition channel and the vertically integrated reconditioning-to-delivery workflow. Scale economies are the primary power here, and they are real, measurable, and growing.

2. Network Effects Weak

Carvana does not benefit from meaningful network effects in the traditional sense. A consumer using Carvana to buy a car does not make the platform more valuable for the next consumer — the transactions are discrete and bilateral, not interdependent. There is a weak data network effect: as Carvana processes more vehicle transactions, its proprietary pricing algorithms and inspection protocols should improve in accuracy, potentially enabling better vehicle acquisition decisions and lower reconditioning cost variance. However, this data advantage is modest compared to platforms with genuine direct or two-sided network effects (like an exchange or marketplace). Carvana's wholesale channel (ADESA) has slightly stronger network effects — more dealer participants improve price discovery — but even here, the effect is limited in a market where Manheim and other wholesale platforms dominate. For practical purposes, network effects are not a significant source of competitive advantage for Carvana.

3. Counter-Positioning Moderate

Carvana's purely digital, no-dealership model represents a genuine counter-positioning threat to traditional franchise dealers, who cannot abandon their physical retail infrastructure without enormous financial and regulatory consequences — franchised dealers are contractually required to maintain physical showrooms by their OEM franchise agreements, and their real estate, sales staff, and service departments represent sunk costs that cannot be easily liquidated. A traditional dealer who tries to fully replicate Carvana's model would be cannibalizing their existing floor traffic, alienating their OEM franchise partner, and competing against a lower-cost structure from a position of higher fixed costs. The rating is only Moderate rather than Strong because CarMax — the most important competitor — is not a franchise dealer and can and is investing to go digital, and because Carvana's model requires its own substantial fixed infrastructure (IRCs, logistics). The counter-positioning is real against the 40,000+ independent and franchise used-car dealers across the US, but not against CarMax or other national players with the capital and strategic intent to respond.

4. Switching Costs Weak

Used-car buying is a transactional, low-frequency, high-consideration purchase. Most consumers buy a car every 4–7 years, and each purchase starts with price comparison across multiple channels — including Carvana, CarMax, local dealers, and private-party marketplaces like CarGurus. There is no meaningful lock-in: consumers can (and do) search for their next vehicle wherever offers the best price and convenience. Carvana does not have a subscription product, embedded software, or recurring revenue stream that would create switching friction. The partial exception is the financing relationship — consumers who financed through Carvana Financial have some friction in switching lenders mid-transaction — but this is a very thin lock-in and is easily overcome. The low switching cost is partially compensated by the customer experience quality (7-day return policy, home delivery, transparent no-negotiation pricing), which drives repeat usage, but this is a branding/NPS benefit rather than a structural switching cost moat.

5. Branding Moderate

Carvana has built one of the most recognizable consumer brands in used automotive retail, largely through its distinctive vending machine aesthetic, its commitment to price transparency and no-negotiation buying, and its highly rated customer experience. Its NPS scores are among the highest in the automotive retail industry, and aided brand awareness among US adults is substantial for a 12-year-old company. The brand creates real value: it reduces customer acquisition cost (organic search, word-of-mouth referrals), enables premium pricing on vehicle sales (consumers pay slightly more to avoid dealership negotiations), and supports F&I attach rates (consumers trust Carvana's financing as a default option). However, the brand is more of a "preference" brand than a "premium pricing" brand in the Helmer sense — consumers are not paying dramatically above market for a Carvana-sourced vehicle the way a luxury consumer pays above fair value for a Hermès bag. The brand is strong relative to the used-car industry but moderate relative to the broader consumer brand universe.

6. Cornered Resource Moderate

Carvana's most important cornered resource is its IRC network — 33 large-format, purpose-built inspection and reconditioning facilities representing billions of dollars of invested capital across strategically chosen geographies. These facilities took years to site, permit, construct, and operationalize; a competitor cannot replicate this network quickly even with unlimited capital, due to real estate availability constraints, regulatory approvals, and the organizational learning required to run them efficiently at scale. The ADESA wholesale auction network (acquired in 2022 for $2.2B) is a second cornered resource — Carvana now controls a significant portion of US wholesale vehicle auction volume, giving it proprietary pricing data and wholesale monetization capability that independent used-car dealers lack. These are real, valuable, non-easily-replicable assets. The reason this is Moderate rather than Strong is that the IRC network, while hard to replicate in full, could be partially replicated by a well-capitalized competitor (e.g., a PE-backed roll-up) over a 5–7 year horizon, and Carvana does not hold a regulatory monopoly or unique IP that physically prevents replication.

7. Process Power Moderate

Carvana has developed proprietary operational capabilities that are genuinely difficult to replicate: its instant offer pricing algorithm (which allows it to bid for customer-owned vehicles in real time with high accuracy), its logistics routing and last-mile delivery system (which coordinates vehicle transport across thousands of daily deliveries nationwide), and its end-to-end digital transaction workflow (which enables a consumer to go from search to signed documents in under 30 minutes). These processes are embedded in years of iteration, proprietary code, operational data, and institutional knowledge that cannot be lifted by hiring a few engineers. However, unlike truly process-powered companies (Toyota's production system, Danaher's DBS), Carvana's processes have not yet been tested across a full economic cycle at scale — the company was still unprofitable through most of its history — and the extent to which the operational advantage is sustainable versus replicable by a determined competitor with sufficient capital and time is genuinely debatable. Process power is present and growing but not yet definitively established as durable.

Summary: Carvana's most durable competitive advantages are Scale Economies (the fixed-cost leverage from the IRC network is the core investment thesis) and Cornered Resource (the IRC/ADESA infrastructure). Counter-Positioning and Process Power are meaningful secondary advantages. The absence of Network Effects and Switching Costs means that if Carvana fails to execute on unit volume growth, it cannot fall back on a recurring revenue base or locked-in customers to sustain profitability.

5. Porter's 5 Forces
1. Threat of New Entrants Moderate

The threat of new entrants at scale is genuinely low — building a national used-car digital platform with its own IRC network and last-mile logistics requires $5B+ of capital, years of operational learning, and a brand strong enough to generate organic consumer demand. No venture-backed startup can replicate this today. However, the "new entrant" threat Carvana actually faces is somewhat different: large incumbents (CarMax with $5B+ revenue, Autonation, Lithia Motors) deciding to invest more aggressively in digital channels are effectively new entrants to the online-pure segment even though they are massive existing players. CarMax's ACO investments and Lithia's Driveway platform represent exactly this threat. The capital barriers are high enough to prevent true startups, but not high enough to prevent the incumbent dealers from pivoting. Overall the threat is Moderate: the capital moat is real, but motivated, capital-rich incumbents are already partially inside the moat.

2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers Moderate

Carvana's primary "suppliers" are the vehicle sellers — consumers and dealers who provide used-car inventory. Unlike a manufacturing business, Carvana cannot dictate supply; it must compete in the open market for vehicle acquisition at prevailing wholesale and retail prices. In a tight inventory environment (as occurred during COVID), acquisition costs spike and margins compress. However, because no single seller represents a meaningful percentage of inventory, and Carvana has multiple acquisition channels (consumer direct, ADESA wholesale, dealer trade-ins, and fleet/rental returns), supplier power is diffuse rather than concentrated. A second supplier consideration is vehicle floorplan financing — the banks and specialty finance companies that lend against inventory — which is more concentrated. Rising floorplan rates in a high-interest-rate environment represent a real cost pressure that Carvana has limited ability to renegotiate. On balance, supplier power is Moderate: the fragmented vehicle acquisition landscape limits individual seller leverage, but macro rate environment and inventory market conditions are real external forces Carvana cannot control.

3. Bargaining Power of Buyers High

Consumers in the used-car market have very high bargaining power relative to the automotive retail industry as a whole. The used-car transaction is high-value, low-frequency, and fully price-transparent in the internet era: a consumer shopping for a 2021 Honda Accord can see every comparable listing on CarGurus, AutoTrader, CarMax.com, and Carvana.com simultaneously in under five minutes. Price comparison is effortless, search costs are near-zero, and there is no meaningful switching cost between platforms. While Carvana's "no-negotiation" pricing model converts this buyer power into a different kind of convenience (eliminating the discomfort of price negotiation), it also means Carvana cannot charge a sustained premium; its prices must be competitive or traffic migrates immediately. The flip side is that buyers cannot extract concessions below Carvana's asking price the way they could at a traditional dealership, but the aggregate power of a completely transparent market for a commoditized asset remains High.

4. Threat of Substitutes Moderate

The functional substitute for buying a used car from Carvana is buying a used car from any other channel: CarMax (physical + digital), a franchise dealer, an independent lot, or a private party via Craigslist/Facebook Marketplace. These alternatives have always existed and will continue to exist; Carvana's value proposition is speed, convenience, and transparency rather than product exclusivity. A longer-horizon substitute is the secular shift toward ride-sharing and subscription mobility (Uber, Lyft, autonomous vehicle fleets), which could structurally reduce per-capita car ownership over the next 10–20 years — though this remains a very long-dated risk. Near-term, the threat from new-car alternatives is partially mitigated by new-car affordability constraints (average new vehicle price ~$48,000) that push buyers toward the used market. On balance, substitutes are Moderate in intensity: the channel is substitutable, but the underlying used-car transaction remains a large and structurally stable category.

5. Competitive Rivalry High

Used automotive retail is intensely competitive. The US used-car market has approximately 40,000 franchise dealers and 40,000+ independent dealers, plus CarMax (~230 stores, ~800K retail units/year), Autonation Digital, Vroom (shrinking but present), and dozens of regional chains. Competition operates on price, selection, financing availability, and increasingly digital convenience. CarMax in particular represents a sophisticated, well-capitalized, and operationally experienced rival that directly benchmarks against Carvana's GPU and unit economics. Rivalry is High because: (1) the product is substantially undifferentiated at the unit level — a 2020 Toyota Camry is the same asset regardless of who sells it; (2) industry growth, while favorable long-term, is cyclical and can turn negative in recessions; (3) competitors with physical infrastructure are investing aggressively in digital capability to narrow Carvana's convenience advantage; (4) exit barriers are low for the 80,000 smaller dealers, meaning irrational competitors can persist on thin margins for extended periods. Carvana's best defense against rivalry is cost structure — as it achieves more units through its fixed IRC base, it can price more competitively than any dealer lacking similar operating leverage.

Summary: The competitive environment for used-car retail is challenging — High rivalry, High buyer power, and Moderate supplier power and substitution risk create a structurally demanding industry. Carvana's investment thesis does not depend on the industry being attractive; it depends on Carvana winning a larger share of an inherently difficult industry by having structurally lower costs and a better consumer experience. That is a credible thesis, but it requires sustained execution over many years in a market where large, well-capitalized incumbents are actively responding.

6. What Drives the Stock?

Carvana is a story stock with extremely high earnings sensitivity to a small number of operating variables. These are the metrics that drive quarterly earnings surprises and determine the multiple re-rating direction:

1. Retail Units Sold (YoY Growth Rate)

Unit growth is the single most important number in every earnings release. At $7,044 GPU per unit, each incremental 10K units/quarter = ~$70M incremental annual GPU. The market's bull case to a $400–500 stock assumes Carvana reaccelerates to 30–50% unit growth sustained for multiple years; any deceleration below ~20–25% YoY growth triggers immediate multiple compression. Q4 2025 at +51% YoY was exceptional; the debate is whether that pace is sustainable as the base gets harder. Bullish: demand pull-forward from used-car affordability crisis. Bearish: macro headwinds, higher rates slowing consumer financing appetite.

2. Gross Profit Per Unit (GPU) — Total and by Segment

GPU is management's primary KPI and the analyst community's most-watched metric. FY2025 GPU of $7,044 compares to a FY2019 peak of ~$3,200 — the improvement reflects vertical integration, F&I maturation, and operational efficiency. Each $100 change in total GPU across 416K units = ~$42M annual EBITDA impact. The sub-components matter too: Retail GPU (~$3,421) tracks used-car pricing and reconditioning efficiency; Wholesale GPU (~$972) reflects ADESA market conditions; F&I GPU (~$2,651) is rate-sensitive and the most opaque. Any print below $6,500 total GPU would likely cause significant sell-side downgrades.

3. EBITDA Margin — Absolute Level and Direction

The margin trajectory from 2.7% (FY23) → 9.6% (FY24) → 10.6% (FY25) → 10.9% (FY26E) is the primary narrative driving the current premium multiple. If EBITDA margin expansion stalls below 11–12%, the market will question whether the scale leverage thesis is real; if margins accelerate toward 13–14%, the stock rerate is enormous. SG&A as % of GPU is the key efficiency metric — management disclosed SG&A per unit declining as volume scales, and any reversal (from customer acquisition cost inflation or logistics inefficiency) is a major negative signal.

4. Used-Car Price Environment (Manheim Used Vehicle Value Index)

Retail GPU is partly a function of the spread between acquisition cost and retail price. When the Manheim index rises, vehicles appreciate in inventory and generate windfall GPU; when it falls, the reverse occurs. The Manheim index is a macro variable Carvana cannot control and is the most common analyst reference for modeling GPU direction. A 5–10% decline in used-car values erases roughly $500–1,000 of retail GPU per unit depending on inventory turn timing. This is particularly relevant given the gradual normalization of pandemic-era vehicle supply shortages.

5. Interest Rates / F&I Securitization Spread

Carvana originates vehicle loans and sells most of them into the ABS (asset-backed securities) market. The profitability of this activity depends on the spread between the rate charged to consumers and the rate at which Carvana can sell the loans. When benchmark rates rise or ABS spreads widen in a risk-off environment, this spread compresses dramatically — as it did in 2022 when F&I contribution turned negative. Conversely, in a rate-cutting cycle or risk-on environment, the spread widens and F&I GPU expands. The sensitivity is approximately $300–600 GPU per unit per 100bps of rate movement, making this the most binary variable in the model for near-term earnings surprises.

6. Balance Sheet / Debt Refinancing Progress

Despite the operational recovery, the $4.45B debt load continues to attract investor attention, particularly for institutions with credit-sensitive mandates. Progress on debt reduction — through FCF generation and potential liability management transactions — is a positive catalyst. Any negative development (covenant breach, refinancing at punitive rates, credit rating action) would trigger an outsized equity reaction given the leverage. As Debt/Equity normalizes toward 38% by FY27E, this overhang fades; but until then, it is a real variable for shorter-duration holders.

Which 2–3 drivers have the most leverage on total revenue? By far the most important variable for near-term EBITDA is Retail Units Sold — every incremental 50,000 units at the FY2025 GPU generates ~$352M of incremental gross profit, which falls mostly to EBITDA since the IRC infrastructure cost is fixed. The second most important is F&I GPU per unit, which is essentially a pure-margin revenue stream; a $500 improvement in F&I GPU across 500K units = $250M of incremental EBITDA. Third is Reconditioning Cost per Unit, which drives Retail GPU: Carvana has guided to declining per-unit recon costs as IRC throughput increases, and each $100 reduction across the base = ~$42M annual EBITDA at current volumes. Importantly, all three levers are interconnected: unit volume growth is the master lever that drives IRC utilization (reducing recon cost) and F&I dollar volume simultaneously.

8. AlphaSense Insights
AlphaSense DR Report — Generated March 16, 2026 · Query: Carvana Co (CVNA) competitive position, used-car market dynamics, F&I economics, management credibility, and long-term unit economics thesis. The Reconditioning Cost Advantage: Real, But Not Permanent. Multiple expert call transcripts in the AlphaSense report (ex-Carvana operational managers, independent used-car dealers, and auto industry consultants) corroborate that Carvana's IRC model has a genuine cost advantage over the traditional used-car reconditioning model — estimated at $400–$600 per unit versus independent dealer reconditioning. The mechanism is straightforward: Carvana's scale allows it to negotiate bulk parts pricing, standardize inspection protocols across thousands of simultaneous vehicles, and amortize fixed equipment and labor over high volumes. One former Carvana IRC manager noted that a fully ramped IRC facility at 60%+ utilization generates recon costs "meaningfully below $1,000 per unit," versus industry averages of $1,400–$1,600. However, the same sources cautioned that this advantage narrows at the unit level as IRC facilities become saturated and Carvana must expand into higher-cost geographies for new facilities — the next set of IRCs will be built in less-favorable real estate markets than the initial batch, which were sited opportunistically during COVID-era commercial real estate dislocations.

CarMax's Competitive Response: More Serious Than Consensus Believes. Several sell-side analyst notes and one expert call from a former CarMax digital strategy executive suggest that CarMax's ACO (Auto Commerce Online) initiative is more advanced than public commentary implies. CarMax can now complete fully digital transactions (no in-person interaction required) in approximately 23 of its top 50 markets, with plans to expand to 100% of markets by 2027. The key insight from the expert call: CarMax's cost structure benefit relative to Carvana is that CarMax's physical stores serve as de facto IRCs — every CarMax location already performs reconditioning on-site — meaning CarMax does not need to build a separate IRC network to operate at Carvana-equivalent economics. CarMax's unit volume of ~800K/year (roughly 2x Carvana's FY2025 level) means CarMax already has the scale to compete on reconditioning cost per unit. The consensus view that Carvana's cost moat is unthreatened may be understated. However, CarMax's omni-channel hybrid model (physical + digital) is structurally higher-cost than Carvana's pure-digital model at the margin, and the full elimination of physical stores is not a credible near-term strategy for CarMax.

F&I Economics: The Opaque Core Profit Driver. Multiple analyst notes flagged the opacity of Carvana's F&I revenue as a key risk and analytical gap. Carvana does not disclose its ABS loss rates, retained interest percentage, or the yield curve assumption embedded in its loan origination economics. What is disclosed is F&I GPU per unit ($2,651 in FY2025) — but this figure combines very different economic activities: (1) the origination spread on financed vehicles (rate-sensitive, volatile), (2) GAP insurance income (relatively stable, tied to finance penetration rate), and (3) service contract income (growing, relatively rate-insensitive). One sell-side note from a major bank estimated that approximately 60–70% of F&I GPU is origination-spread income, meaning roughly $1,600–1,850 per unit is directly sensitive to interest rates and ABS market conditions. The expert community consensus is that the ABS market has been favorable for Carvana in FY2024–25 and that normalization or tightening could reduce F&I GPU by $300–500 per unit — a meaningful compression that management's guidance language of "substantial" FY2026 growth does not appear to fully account for.

Leadership Credibility and the Ernie Garcia III Question. Governance concerns around Ernie Garcia III (CEO) and Ernie Garcia II (father, controlling shareholder, and former founder of DriveTime — a subprime used-car dealer) are consistently raised in the AlphaSense source base. Garcia II controls a majority of voting power through his Class B shares, and the interrelationship between Carvana and DriveTime (which was a major customer for Carvana in its early years and still has some business interactions) creates related-party transaction risk. Several institutional investor commentary notes flagged this as a "governance haircut" on the multiple. However, the operational credibility of Garcia III's management team has been substantially restored by the FY2025 execution — the turnaround from near-bankruptcy to a $65B market cap in two years, while generating record GPU and meeting or exceeding every major operational commitment, is an almost unprecedented demonstration of execution capability. The expert consensus: governance risk is real and worth a 10–15% multiple discount; operational execution risk has materially diminished.

Long-Term Market Share Runway: The 3M Unit Vision. Multiple industry analyst sources corroborate that the 3-million-unit long-term target is mathematically credible given the ~40M unit US used-car market, but that the pathway requires 10–12 years of sustained above-market growth at current rates. One automotive industry consultant estimated that reaching 3M units requires Carvana to own approximately 7–8% of the US used-car market — a level that would make it by far the dominant national player and would require meaningfully displacing both independent dealers and CarMax. The sources are divided on whether this is achievable: optimists point to the secular digitization of commerce (citing Chewy's 20%+ share of the US pet food market as an analogy for digital-native category disruption); pessimists note that the car is a higher-consideration, more variable, and more geographically heterogeneous product than most e-commerce categories. The investor debate on this point remains live and unresolved — it is the central source of outcome dispersion in the CVNA investment thesis.

Key Long-Term Investor Questions. Based on the AlphaSense source synthesis, the questions that sophisticated long-term institutional investors are most focused on are: (1) At what point does GPU stabilize, and is the FY2025 level of $7,044 a durable baseline or an inflection year that benefits from one-time favorable conditions? (2) How does Carvana's model hold up in a genuine recession — the company has never managed through a full downturn with its current cost structure and the ADESA overlay? (3) What is the sustainable EBITDA margin at 1M, 1.5M, and 3M units, and does the IRC utilization math actually work in practice (i.e., can the company maintain consistent reconditioning quality at much higher throughput without quality incidents)? (4) Will the Garcia family governance structure ever change, and is there a scenario where Garcia II's stake becomes a source of value-destructive related-party decisions?

12. Is This a Theia Investment?

Theia seeks small-cap companies (<$5B market cap) that are not well-covered by sell-side analysts and are strongly aligned on the core internet finance / blockchain thesis with wide outcome dispersion. Here is how CVNA screens:

(i) Market Cap Bucket: $65.7B Weak

Large Cap — well outside Theia's sweet spot. At $65.7B market cap, Carvana is one of the largest used-car companies in the world and is covered by 23+ sell-side analysts across every major bank. The informational edge available to a small, nimble investor is minimal in this name. Theia's stated focus is sub-$5B, ideally sub-$2B, where institutional coverage is thin and mispricing opportunities are structurally more available.

(ii) Analyst Coverage: 23 broker contributors Weak

Well-covered — every major bank (Goldman, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, BofA, Wells Fargo, Citi, Barclays, etc.) covers CVNA with dedicated analyst teams. Average consensus rating of 1.35 (Overweight) reflects near-unanimous bullishness, which itself is a contrarian flag: with 23 OW ratings, the "positive surprise" upside from analyst upgrades is limited. Short interest of 9.86% of float reflects the presence of sophisticated short-sellers who have done deep work on the other side of the trade. No informational alpha here for a small fund.

(iii) Internet Finance / Blockchain Thesis Alignment: Tangential Weak

Carvana is a digital-native e-commerce platform, which places it adjacent to internet commerce, but it has no meaningful blockchain exposure and limited financial services depth. The F&I component (originating and securitizing vehicle loans) creates a fintech-adjacent quality, but this is standard automotive finance practice, not an internet finance innovation. Theia's core thesis centers on the digitization of financial infrastructure — crypto exchanges, digital banks, capital markets technology, blockchain-native financial services. Carvana's thesis is fundamentally about used-car retail disruption, which is directionally aligned with digital commerce but not aligned with Theia's specific internet finance focus.

(iv) Outcome Dispersion: Wide Strong

This is the one area where CVNA genuinely scores well for Theia's framework. The 5-scenario model produces implied FY2028E share prices ranging from $62 (Bear Severe) to $922 (Bull Severe) — a 15x spread. The base case barely covers current price (+8.6% upside from $300.15), and the probability-weighted intrinsic value at 30% CoC is ~$274, implying the stock is modestly overvalued at current levels. Wide dispersion is present but is a function of leverage and operational volatility rather than informational asymmetry — the reasons for the wide range are well-understood and well-modeled by the large sell-side analyst community.

NO — Not a Theia Investment

Carvana fails three of four Theia screening criteria. At $65.7B market cap with 23 sell-side analysts and tangential alignment with the internet finance thesis, there is no structural edge available to a small, concentrated fund running a differentiated information approach. The stock is intellectually interesting (the 3M-unit thesis is one of the most debated investment ideas in the US equity market) and the operational turnaround is genuinely impressive, but interesting ideas at large-cap valuations with saturation coverage do not belong in Theia's portfolio.
