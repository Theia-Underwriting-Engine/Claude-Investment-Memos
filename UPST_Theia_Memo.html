Upstart Holdings, Inc. | Preliminary Memo for Theia
1. Business Overview
Upstart Holdings, Inc. (UPST) is an AI-powered consumer lending marketplace founded in 2012 and headquartered in San Mateo, CA. Co-founded by Dave Girouard (CEO, ex-Google), the company's core thesis is that traditional FICO-based credit underwriting is a blunt instrument that simultaneously rejects creditworthy borrowers and approves ones who will default — and that a machine-learning model trained on hundreds of non-traditional variables can dramatically outperform it. The platform connects consumers seeking personal, auto, home equity, and small-dollar loans with a network of 100+ bank and credit union lending partners, as well as institutional capital, earning fees at origination and on an ongoing servicing basis.
Upstart does not primarily lend its own capital — it is a fee-based marketplace that generated $950M in fee revenue on $11.0B of loan originations in FY2025 (a blended take rate of ~8.6%). The AI model currently incorporates over 2,500 variables and is trained on ~104 million repayment events, generating approximately 105,000 new training data points per day. The company's 2025 internal study found its model approves 43% more borrowers at 33% lower average APR than FICO-based underwriting — the core value proposition for both borrowers and lenders. After two difficult years (FY2022–2023) caused by a sharp deterioration in institutional funding, EBITDA turned positive in 2024 and reached $230M on an adjusted basis in FY2025, with management expressing high confidence in a "very strong 2026" driven by new product commercialization (auto, HELOC) and conversion rate improvements from AI model upgrades.

3. Helmer's 7 Powers
1. Scale EconomiesModerate
Upstart's model development costs (engineering, compute, data infrastructure) are largely fixed and spread across an ever-growing origination base — $11.0B in FY2025 vs. $1.3B in FY2020. The cost to underwrite the marginal loan is essentially zero once the model exists. However, the primary value of scale is in data accumulation (see Cornered Resource), not unit cost reduction in the traditional sense. Loan-level variable costs (fraud checks, verification) remain, limiting pure scale economy depth.
2. Network EffectsModerate
Upstart benefits from a data network effect: more loans originated → more repayment events → better models → higher approval rates and lower APRs → more borrowers and lending partners → still more loans. This is a genuine flywheel but operates on a years-long lag rather than instantly. The two-sided marketplace structure (borrowers + lending partners) creates indirect network effects — more lenders compete for the same borrower pool, driving down borrower rates and improving conversion. These are real but weaker than direct social network effects; a smaller competitor can still underwrite loans with reasonable accuracy at modest scale.
3. Counter-PositioningStrong
This is arguably Upstart's single most durable advantage. Large banks own vast loan repayment datasets but cannot deploy them in the way Upstart does without: (a) admitting their FICO-based underwriting has been wrong for decades, (b) accepting the pricing and model control that Upstart's architecture requires, and (c) cannibalizing their existing credit card and personal loan businesses. A JPMorgan Chase could not partner with Upstart without funding its own disintermediation — so they don't. Meanwhile, their incumbent credit underwriting continues to "approve the wrong people at the wrong price" by Upstart's framing. The counter-position is structural, not circumstantial.
4. Switching CostsModerate
Lending partners (banks/CUs) that integrate Upstart's API into their loan origination workflows face meaningful switching costs: re-building or re-integrating a competing underwriting stack, retraining staff, and accepting a period of performance uncertainty during transition. Repeat borrowers on Upstart.com (who have positive credit history with the platform) get systematically better rates on re-application, creating mild borrower lock-in. However, consumers face essentially zero switching cost if they choose to shop elsewhere, and smaller lending partners can exit if performance disappoints.
5. BrandingWeak
Upstart has built consumer awareness ("Upstart.com" as a lending destination) but does not command significant brand premium or habitual purchasing. Most borrowers reach Upstart through performance marketing, comparison sites (NerdWallet, LendingTree), or bank partner referrals — not brand loyalty. The Upstart brand conveys "AI-powered loan approval" to informed consumers but lacks the broad consumer recognition of a SoFi or even a LendingClub. Branding is not a meaningful competitive moat here.
6. Cornered ResourceStrong
Upstart's training dataset — ~104 million repayment events, 2,500+ non-traditional variables, and ~30 proprietary ML models — is a genuinely non-replicable asset. A competitor starting today would need 10+ years of origination and repayment history across macro cycles before their model would approach comparable accuracy. Incumbents have loan data but lack the diversity of non-credit variables (employment, income trajectory, cost of living, bank transaction behavior) that Upstart uses. The dataset compounds in value as more loans are originated, and the data generates its own training data — unlike general-purpose AI where data can be licensed. Additionally, the proprietary Upstart Macro Index (UMI), calibrated in real-time, represents an institutional knowledge resource that cannot be bought or copied quickly.
7. Process PowerStrong
Over 13 years, Upstart has built an organizational machine for AI-driven credit: 60% of engineers weekly active on LLM-powered development tools, 700+ custom internal GPTs, a culture that continuously deploys model improvements (Model 22 as of Q2 2025 vs. Model 1 in 2012), and a process for spinning up new product categories (auto, HELOC) by training foundational credit models on cross-category data. The ability to take HELOC automated approvals from <1% to ~20% in four months, and to reduce end-to-end loan pricing latency by 30% via parallelization, reflects embedded organizational capability that cannot be replicated by hiring or acquiring. This is a process organization built around AI iteration, not a technology that can be licensed.
The strongest moats are Counter-Positioning (incumbents rationally cannot copy the model), Cornered Resource (104M repayment events + 2,500 non-traditional variables = years of irreplicable training data), and Process Power (organizational machine for continuous AI improvement). The data flywheel creates a secondary Network Effect, but the primary moat is structural competitive positioning rather than network lock-in.
4. Porter's 5 Forces
1. Threat of New EntrantsModerate
Building an AI lending marketplace requires no physical capital, which lowers entry barriers structurally. However, acquiring lending partner relationships (banks/CUs require regulatory comfort, performance track records, and integration investment), building compliant consumer acquisition channels, and — critically — accumulating enough repayment data to train accurate models each require years and tens of millions of dollars. A well-funded startup could enter personal lending, but matching Upstart's 104M-event training set and 100+ lending partner network would take a decade under favorable conditions. Regulatory scrutiny of AI lending models (fair lending, CFPB oversight) further raises the bar.
2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers (Capital & Funding)High
Upstart's "suppliers" are its capital providers — bank/CU lending partners and institutional investors who purchase or fund the loans originated on the platform. In 2022, when institutional funding tightened dramatically amid rate hikes, Upstart's revenue collapsed 67% YoY — the defining illustration of funding supplier power. Concentration remains a risk: the top 3 lending partners accounted for 83% of loans originated and 61% of total revenue in FY2025. Management has worked to mitigate this through committed capital arrangements (>50% of platform funding now committed, 100% retention of private credit partners, September 2025 ABS oversubscribed), but concentration risk remains elevated. Funding is the single largest structural vulnerability for the business model.
3. Bargaining Power of Buyers (Borrowers)Moderate
Consumers face almost zero switching costs and have full price transparency through comparison sites. However, Upstart's model genuinely delivers lower rates for a meaningful cohort (43% more approvals, 33% lower APR than FICO alternatives) — borrowers who would have been rejected elsewhere don't have an equivalent alternative, dampening their bargaining power. For super-prime borrowers who could get comparable rates from SoFi or LendingClub, Upstart competes on rate and UX, not lock-in. On net, the borrower side is moderately competitive but not structurally adverse since Upstart's value prop is differentiated rate pricing.
4. Threat of SubstitutesHigh
Personal loans are a commoditized product structurally. Credit cards, BNPL, home equity withdrawal, 401(k) loans, personal loans from banks, and marketplace lenders are all substitutes for the Upstart personal loan. For debt consolidation (the primary use case), a balance transfer credit card is a near-perfect substitute. The substitute threat is somewhat mitigated for borrowers who don't qualify elsewhere (Upstart's AI-approved "near prime" cohort), but for the large swath of prime borrowers, alternative sources of capital are readily available and often equally priced.
5. Competitive RivalryHigh
The U.S. consumer lending market is intensely competitive. LendingClub, SoFi, Avant, Prosper, and dozens of bank/CU direct-origination programs compete for the same personal loan borrowers. SoFi benefits from a banking license and low-cost deposit funding; LendingClub has its own bank charter. In Q3 2025, both LendingClub and SoFi reportedly grew origination volumes while Upstart's model tightened due to UMI signals — a real-world illustration of competitive dynamics. In auto (Upstart's emerging vertical), DriveTime, Capital One Auto, and dealer-embedded financing compete. Rivalry is high across all product categories and is unlikely to diminish as the market grows.
The competitive environment is challenging: funding supplier concentration is a genuine existential risk demonstrated by history (2022), substitute products are ubiquitous, and rivalry is intense. The company is somewhat insulated by its counter-positioning and data moat, but neither eliminates the need to continuously outperform on model accuracy to justify the lending partner ecosystem's continued participation.
5. What Drives the Stock?
  1. 1. Loan Origination Volume / Transaction Volume ($B)
    The single most-watched metric. FY2025 volume was $11.0B (+86% YoY); consensus calls for ~$16–17B in FY2026. Each $1B of volume at a blended 8.6% take rate = ~$86M of incremental fee revenue. Volume is the product of: (a) rate inquiries, (b) conversion rate, and (c) average loan size. Any quarter where volume misses — as it did in Q3 2025 when UMI rose and conversion fell from 23.9% to 20.6% — triggers severe stock reactions due to the high operating leverage embedded in the fee-based model.
  2. 2. Conversion Rate (Rate Inquiries → Funded Loans)
    The conversion rate is the most model-sensitive metric and the primary bear/bull toggle. At 23.9% in Q2 2025, Upstart beat estimates significantly; at 20.6% in Q3, the miss was sudden and model-driven (UMI tightening). Bullish scenario: Model 22 improvements + marketing AI driving conversion toward 25%+. Bearish scenario: macro deterioration pushes UMI above 1.5, collapsing conversion to sub-15% (as occurred in 2022). This is the metric to watch every quarter.
  3. 3. New Product Commercialization (Auto and HELOC)
    Auto + HELOC + small dollar are collectively ~12% of originations today but 300%+ YoY growth. If auto retail reaches meaningful scale (~$1B+ quarterly) and HELOC automation reaches personal-loan-level efficiency (currently scaling from <1% to 20% automated approvals in 4 months), total addressable origination volume could be 5–10x current levels. Management called auto "a contributor in 2026." Third-party capital deals for new products are the enabling catalyst — if they close on schedule, balance sheet constraints lift and new product scaling can accelerate. Quarterly new product origination share is the key tracking metric.
  4. 4. Take Rate & Contribution Margin
    Management deliberately moderated take rates beginning Q4 2025 (contribution margin guided to 53% vs. 57–58% prior quarters) to invest in origination volume and customer lifetime value. This is a lever they can pull in either direction. Bulls see this as rational volume investment; bears see margin compression. The blended take rate (fee revenue / transaction volume) has been ~8.5–9.0% historically; any sustained compression below 8% would indicate competitive pricing pressure and would be a negative signal.
  5. 5. Funding Mix and Balance Sheet Normalization
    Upstart held ~$1.2B of loans on its own balance sheet in Q3 2025 (primarily R&D-stage new product loans), representing significant capital consumption and credit risk. Management's guidance to close third-party capital deals for auto and HELOC would release this capital, improve FCF, and remove the structural risk of credit losses from new, immature product models. Investors will track: (a) timing of committed capital deal announcements for new products, and (b) trajectory of loans held on balance sheet. If balance sheet grows further without offsetting third-party deals, it signals funding challenges and elevates credit risk.
  6. 6. Macro / Credit Conditions (UMI)
    Upstart's Upstart Macro Index (UMI = 1.39 as of Dec 31, 2025) is the company's proprietary real-time macro calibration metric. A UMI of 1.39 means macro adds ~39% incremental credit risk vs. baseline. When UMI rises, the model tightens automatically — conversion falls, volume misses, stock gets punished. A declining UMI (improving credit conditions) is the largest potential bull catalyst; CEO Girouard noted that improving repayment speeds and easing consumption growth could signal "a long-anticipated tailwind." Rate cuts from the Fed are also unambiguously positive: lower consumer borrowing costs, improved conversion, lower investor return requirements.
