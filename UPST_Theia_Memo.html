Upstart Holdings, Inc. | Preliminary Memo for Theia
1. Business Overview
Upstart Holdings, Inc. (UPST) is an AI-powered consumer lending marketplace founded in 2012 and headquartered in San Mateo, CA. Co-founded by Dave Girouard (CEO, ex-Google), the company's core thesis is that traditional FICO-based credit underwriting is a blunt instrument that simultaneously rejects creditworthy borrowers and approves ones who will default — and that a machine-learning model trained on hundreds of non-traditional variables can dramatically outperform it. The platform connects consumers seeking personal, auto, home equity, and small-dollar loans with a network of 100+ bank and credit union lending partners, as well as institutional capital, earning fees at origination and on an ongoing servicing basis.
Upstart does not primarily lend its own capital — it is a fee-based marketplace that generated $950M in fee revenue on $11.0B of loan originations in FY2025 (a blended take rate of ~8.6%). The AI model currently incorporates over 2,500 variables and is trained on ~104 million repayment events, generating approximately 105,000 new training data points per day. The company's 2025 internal study found its model approves 43% more borrowers at 33% lower average APR than FICO-based underwriting — the core value proposition for both borrowers and lenders. After two difficult years (FY2022–2023) caused by a sharp deterioration in institutional funding, EBITDA turned positive in 2024 and reached $230M on an adjusted basis in FY2025, with management expressing high confidence in a "very strong 2026" driven by new product commercialization (auto, HELOC) and conversion rate improvements from AI model upgrades.

3. Helmer's 7 Powers
1. Scale EconomiesModerate
Upstart's model development costs — the engineering team, GPU compute, data infrastructure, and compliance frameworks — are largely fixed and spread across an ever-growing origination base: $11.0B in FY2025 vs. $1.3B in FY2020. The cost to underwrite the marginal loan is essentially zero once the model exists; Upstart doesn't need to hire another data scientist every time a new borrower applies. This creates real operating leverage: as volume grows, the fixed cost per loan originated falls, which is why EBITDA margins expanded sharply from deeply negative to 22%+ adjusted in just two years. However, Upstart's scale advantages are primarily captured through data accumulation (see Cornered Resource below) rather than traditional manufacturing-style cost curves. Loan-level variable costs (third-party fraud checks, income verification API calls, automated soft-credit pulls) still scale with origination volume, which tempers the depth of pure scale economies. The scale moat is real but modest relative to the data moat.
2. Network EffectsModerate
Upstart benefits from a data network effect: more loans originated → more repayment events feed back into model training → better risk separation → higher approval rates and lower APRs offered to borrowers → more borrowers choose Upstart over competitors → more lending partners want access to that flow → still more loans originated. This is a genuine flywheel, but it operates on a years-long lag (repayment events on a 3–5 year personal loan take 3–5 years to accumulate), not the immediate viral loops of a social network. The two-sided marketplace structure — borrowers on one side, bank and credit union lending partners on the other — also creates indirect network effects: as more lenders compete to deploy capital through Upstart, they bid down the rates offered to borrowers, which makes Upstart's rates more attractive, which draws more borrowers, which attracts still more lenders. These are real reinforcing dynamics, but they are weaker than direct social network effects. A well-funded new entrant with $500M could start originating loans and begin accumulating training data within months — the data moat's advantage is its depth and time-in-market, not an inherent network lock-in.
3. Counter-PositioningStrong
Counter-positioning — in Helmer's framework — means the incumbent cannot rationally copy the challenger's business model because doing so would damage or destroy their existing business. This is arguably Upstart's single most durable structural advantage. Large banks (JPMorgan, Wells Fargo, Bank of America) theoretically own decades of proprietary loan repayment data and could attempt to build their own AI underwriting engine. But doing so would require: (a) publicly admitting their FICO-based underwriting has been mispricing credit risk for decades, creating legal and reputational exposure; (b) accepting that an AI model may approve borrowers their human credit officers would reject, which their risk committees — under OCC and Federal Reserve oversight — are culturally and structurally resistant to; and (c) potentially cannibalizing their highest-margin products (revolving credit cards, overdraft lines) by offering borrowers better-priced alternatives. A JPMorgan Chase that used Upstart's model would effectively be funding the disintermediation of its own credit card business. This is why the biggest banks don't partner with Upstart — not because they couldn't afford to build it, but because doing so is strategically irrational given what they already have. Upstart's strategy is explicitly to serve the "long tail" of 1,000+ regional banks and credit unions that lack the engineering talent and capital budget to build AI underwriting in-house, effectively arming the fragmented banking ecosystem against the largest institutions.
4. Switching CostsModerate
Lending partners — the banks and credit unions that route their loan originations through Upstart's API — face meaningful switching costs once integrated. The integration itself requires connecting Upstart's API to the bank's core loan origination system, training loan officers on a new workflow, satisfying the bank's risk committee and compliance team with Upstart's fair-lending validation documentation, and enduring a multi-month "live deployment" period where the new model's performance is uncertain. Expert calls suggest these sales cycles routinely take 12–18 months, and the switching cost once in production is an equivalent 12–18 month re-integration process — plus the reputational and operational risk of replacing a model mid-stream. On the borrower side, switching costs are genuinely low: consumers face essentially zero friction shopping Upstart against LendingClub or a bank directly. However, Upstart's repeat borrower program — where prior borrowers with positive repayment history receive systematically lower rates on re-application (because the model has direct, verified payment history, not just FICO proxies) — creates a mild economic lock-in. The combination makes lending partner stickiness the more important switching cost dynamic for the B2B revenue base.
5. BrandingWeak
Brand power — in Helmer's framework — means the brand itself commands a pricing premium or drives habitual repeat purchasing without the need for comparable product quality. Upstart has built meaningful consumer awareness (Upstart.com as a lending destination, "AI-powered loan approval" as a differentiated positioning), but it does not command a meaningful brand premium or generate habitual consumer loyalty. The reality is that most borrowers arrive through performance marketing spend, loan comparison aggregators (NerdWallet, LendingTree, Credit Karma), or referrals from bank partners — not brand recall or emotional affinity. When a borrower needs a personal loan, they go to the aggregator that shows them the best rate, not to Upstart specifically. Relative to SoFi, which has built a recognizable consumer brand with sports naming rights and a broader financial product suite that creates cross-sell stickiness, Upstart's brand is a minor competitive factor. This is not a moat; it is simply a marketing presence.
6. Cornered ResourceStrong
This is the deepest structural moat in the business. Upstart's training dataset — approximately 104 million individual borrower repayment events accumulated over 13 years, incorporating 2,500+ variables per loan — is a genuinely non-replicable asset. To understand why this matters: machine learning models for credit underwriting are only as accurate as their training data. You cannot buy this data, scrape it from the internet, or synthesize it. To replicate it, a competitor would need to originate millions of actual loans, wait the full 3–5 year repayment cycle for each cohort, and observe outcomes across multiple macro cycles (bull markets, recessions, rate cycles) before their training set would be large and diverse enough to challenge Upstart's accuracy. Even JPMorgan or Wells Fargo, which have decades of loan data, lack the 2,500+ non-credit variables (educational trajectory, income growth patterns, bank transaction behavior, cost-of-living relative to income) that Upstart's model incorporates — because traditional banks were never trying to collect this data. Upstart's Model 25, launched in late 2025, introduced an additional breakthrough: it incorporates outcomes for borrowers Upstart did not fund — by matching its rejected applicants to similar loans originated elsewhere in the market and observing their actual performance. This expanded the training universe dramatically, yielding a 172.2% accuracy advantage over benchmark FICO-based models, with accuracy improvements more than 100x larger for the previously-unobserved borrower segments. The Upstart Macro Index (UMI) — a proprietary real-time calibration metric that adjusts the model's risk thresholds based on current macroeconomic conditions — represents an additional institutional knowledge resource that no external party can replicate without decades of model iteration across economic cycles.
7. Process PowerStrong
Process power — in Helmer's framework — is embedded organizational capability that competitors cannot replicate simply by hiring talent or writing a check, because it is a product of institutional muscle memory accumulated over years of iteration. Over 13 years, Upstart has built a remarkably specialized machine for AI-driven credit: 60% of engineers are weekly-active users of LLM-powered development tools; over 700 custom internal GPTs have been built for specific operational tasks; and the company has progressed from Model 1 in 2012 to Model 25 in late 2025, iterating through 25 generations of architectural improvements. The majority of Upstart's ML model team holds doctorate-level degrees in statistics, mathematics, computer science, economics, or physics — talent that traditional credit unions and regional banks fundamentally lack the compensation structure and mission appeal to attract. The organizational process shows up in quantifiable outcomes: HELOC automated approval rates went from near-zero to 20% in four months; the first end-to-end instantly-approved auto refinance was executed in a single nine-minute session; voice LLM systems deployed in late 2025 are autonomously handling manual verification calls while reducing verification-related default rates by 0.8%. None of these capabilities can be replicated by a competitor buying an off-the-shelf LLM API or hiring a team of data scientists — they require the institutional knowledge of what variables matter, how models fail in live deployment, and how to build compliance frameworks that regulators accept. This is a process organization built around AI iteration, and it deepens its own moat every quarter it operates.
The strongest moats are Counter-Positioning (incumbents rationally cannot copy the model), Cornered Resource (104M repayment events + 2,500 non-traditional variables = years of irreplicable training data), and Process Power (organizational machine for continuous AI improvement). The data flywheel creates a secondary Network Effect, but the primary moat is structural competitive positioning rather than network lock-in.
4. Porter's 5 Forces
1. Threat of New EntrantsModerate
Building an AI lending marketplace requires no physical capital, no branch network, and no banking license — which structurally lowers entry barriers compared to traditional banking. A well-funded startup could theoretically launch a consumer lending platform within 12 months. However, several durable barriers raise the effective entry cost far above zero. First, acquiring bank and credit union lending partners is a multi-year process: these institutions require that new AI underwriting vendors demonstrate regulatory compliance with the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (ECOA) and fair lending laws, provide 12+ months of live origination track records, pass their own risk committee review, and complete API integration into the bank's core operating system — a process that typically takes 12–18 months per partner. Second, consumer acquisition at scale requires either expensive performance marketing spend (which Upstart itself continues to invest in) or integration with major aggregators like Credit Karma, which requires demonstrating competitive approval rates and speed. Third — and most critically — accumulating the repayment data necessary to build an accurate underwriting model requires actually originating millions of loans and waiting years for borrowers to repay or default. A new entrant in 2026 starting from zero would need a decade of multi-cycle repayment history before their model could challenge Upstart's 172.2% accuracy advantage. Regulatory scrutiny of AI lending decisions (CFPB oversight, state-level fair lending enforcement) further raises compliance costs and slows the iteration cycle for new entrants who lack established compliance frameworks.
2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers (Capital & Funding)High
Unlike a software business, Upstart cannot operate without capital — specifically, banks, credit unions, and institutional investors who are willing to fund or purchase the loans originated on its platform. These capital providers are Upstart's critical "suppliers," and their bargaining power is extremely high because they hold the key input that makes every transaction possible. In 2022, when the Federal Reserve raised interest rates by 425 basis points in 12 months, institutional investors demanded higher returns, many lending partners paused or reduced their loan purchase commitments, and Upstart's origination volume collapsed 73% peak-to-trough — its revenue fell 67% YoY and the company posted deep GAAP losses. This was not a business model failure; it was a pure funding liquidity crisis that illustrated exactly how exposed the marketplace model is when capital suppliers exercise their power. As of FY2025, concentration has actually increased rather than decreased: the top 3 lending partners accounted for 83% of loans originated and 61% of total revenue. Management has worked to mitigate this through "committed capital" structures — contractual agreements where bank partners commit to purchase a defined volume of loans over a set period at agreed-upon economics — and by building a more diverse institutional investor base (ABS structures, private credit funds). As of Q3 2025, over 50% of platform funding was under committed arrangements, and the September 2025 ABS deal was oversubscribed. But concentration remains a structural vulnerability; Upstart cannot "own" its capital providers the way a bank that holds its own balance sheet can. Funding is the single largest existential risk for this business model.
3. Bargaining Power of Buyers (Borrowers)Moderate
Consumer borrowers have almost zero switching costs — with one click on LendingTree or NerdWallet, a borrower can simultaneously receive offers from Upstart, LendingClub, SoFi, Avant, and their own bank, comparing rates in real time. This full price transparency gives borrowers essentially perfect information and no friction to switch. However, buyer power is moderated significantly by what Upstart's model actually delivers for a large segment of borrowers. Upstart's AI approves 43% more borrowers than FICO-based underwriting would, at 33% lower average APRs, specifically for the "near prime" and "thin file" borrower cohort — people with limited credit history, non-traditional income sources, or recent life transitions who are penalized by FICO's blunt statistical approach. For these borrowers, Upstart may be the only lender offering competitive terms; alternatives are either rejection or a payday loan at 300%+ APR. This dramatically reduces buyer bargaining power for the ~40% of Upstart's volume that is genuinely underserved by traditional underwriting. For the other ~60% — prime borrowers who could get comparable terms from SoFi, LendingClub, or their bank — Upstart competes on rate and user experience rather than uniqueness, and buyer bargaining power is higher. The net assessment is moderate: the "captive" near-prime borrower segment stabilizes the marketplace even when competition intensifies for prime borrowers.
4. Threat of SubstitutesHigh
A "substitute" in Porter's framework is not a competing personal loan lender — it is any product that fulfills the same underlying borrower need (access to cash) through a different mechanism entirely. Personal loans are a deeply substitutable product. The primary use case is debt consolidation (rolling high-interest credit card balances into a single lower-rate installment loan), and for this purpose a 0% balance transfer credit card is a near-perfect substitute — and widely available to prime borrowers. For home-improvement spending, a HELOC or cash-out refinance substitutes. For emergency cash, a 401(k) hardship withdrawal, a paycheck advance product, or BNPL at the point of purchase can substitute. For prime borrowers with strong bank relationships, a personal line of credit from their existing bank eliminates the need to shop a marketplace entirely. The substitute threat is partially mitigated for the near-prime cohort: a borrower who can only access funds through a 36% APR credit card or a payday lender has no meaningful substitute for the 18–22% APR Upstart offers. But for the prime and super-prime market — which represents the most profitable borrowers with the lowest default risk — the substitutability is high and growing. Upstart's auto (secured by vehicle) and HELOC (secured by real estate) products are in some ways a strategic response to substitute risk: as loan size grows and collateral is introduced, the product becomes harder to substitute with unsecured alternatives.
5. Competitive RivalryHigh
The U.S. unsecured consumer lending market is intensely competitive, and the dynamics are structurally unfavorable for Upstart in the short run. Direct marketplace competitors include LendingClub (which acquired Radius Bank in 2020 and now funds loans at deposit cost — meaning it can undercut Upstart on rate for the same borrower profile), SoFi (which targets "doctors and lawyers," i.e., super-prime borrowers with high income and pristine credit, and prices loans cheaply off its $21B deposit base — a structural funding advantage Upstart cannot replicate without a bank charter), Avant, Prosper, and Upgrade, plus the direct-origination consumer lending programs of thousands of banks and credit unions. The competitive asymmetry matters: a bank with a $10B deposit franchise that costs 2.5% to maintain can offer personal loans at 10–12% APR and still make money. Upstart, funding at wholesale institutional rates (4–6%+), must charge enough spread to cover its funding cost, origination fees, and credit losses — which pushes it toward the near-prime segment where deposit-funded banks are less willing to compete. In Q3 2025, when Upstart's Upstart Macro Index (a proprietary real-time measure of prevailing macro credit risk) rose and triggered the model to tighten approvals, both LendingClub and SoFi grew origination volumes while Upstart's fell — a concrete illustration of how competitive rivalry manifests when Upstart's credit risk sensors cause it to pull back before competitors do. In auto lending (Upstart's emerging second vertical), competition is even more intense: Capital One Auto, Ally Financial, dealer-captive financing (Ford Credit, GM Financial), and regional bank auto desks compete intensely on price with deep capital bases. The auto market's advantage for Upstart is differentiated underwriting speed and automation, not unique access to capital.
The competitive environment is challenging: funding supplier concentration is a genuine existential risk demonstrated by history (2022), substitute products are ubiquitous, and rivalry is intense. The company is somewhat insulated by its counter-positioning and data moat, but neither eliminates the need to continuously outperform on model accuracy to justify the lending partner ecosystem's continued participation.
5. What Drives the Stock?
  1. 1. Loan Origination Volume / Transaction Volume ($B)
    The single most-watched metric. FY2025 volume was $11.0B (+86% YoY); consensus calls for ~$16–17B in FY2026. Each $1B of volume at a blended 8.6% take rate = ~$86M of incremental fee revenue. Volume is the product of: (a) rate inquiries, (b) conversion rate, and (c) average loan size. Any quarter where volume misses — as it did in Q3 2025 when UMI rose and conversion fell from 23.9% to 20.6% — triggers severe stock reactions due to the high operating leverage embedded in the fee-based model.
  2. 2. Conversion Rate (Rate Inquiries → Funded Loans)
    The conversion rate is the most model-sensitive metric and the primary bear/bull toggle. At 23.9% in Q2 2025, Upstart beat estimates significantly; at 20.6% in Q3, the miss was sudden and model-driven (UMI tightening). Bullish scenario: Model 22 improvements + marketing AI driving conversion toward 25%+. Bearish scenario: macro deterioration pushes UMI above 1.5, collapsing conversion to sub-15% (as occurred in 2022). This is the metric to watch every quarter.
  3. 3. New Product Commercialization (Auto and HELOC)
    Auto + HELOC + small dollar are collectively ~12% of originations today but 300%+ YoY growth. If auto retail reaches meaningful scale (~$1B+ quarterly) and HELOC automation reaches personal-loan-level efficiency (currently scaling from <1% to 20% automated approvals in 4 months), total addressable origination volume could be 5–10x current levels. Management called auto "a contributor in 2026." Third-party capital deals for new products are the enabling catalyst — if they close on schedule, balance sheet constraints lift and new product scaling can accelerate. Quarterly new product origination share is the key tracking metric.
  4. 4. Take Rate & Contribution Margin
    Management deliberately moderated take rates beginning Q4 2025 (contribution margin guided to 53% vs. 57–58% prior quarters) to invest in origination volume and customer lifetime value. This is a lever they can pull in either direction. Bulls see this as rational volume investment; bears see margin compression. The blended take rate (fee revenue / transaction volume) has been ~8.5–9.0% historically; any sustained compression below 8% would indicate competitive pricing pressure and would be a negative signal.
  5. 5. Funding Mix and Balance Sheet Normalization
    Upstart held ~$1.2B of loans on its own balance sheet in Q3 2025 (primarily R&D-stage new product loans), representing significant capital consumption and credit risk. Management's guidance to close third-party capital deals for auto and HELOC would release this capital, improve FCF, and remove the structural risk of credit losses from new, immature product models. Investors will track: (a) timing of committed capital deal announcements for new products, and (b) trajectory of loans held on balance sheet. If balance sheet grows further without offsetting third-party deals, it signals funding challenges and elevates credit risk.
  6. 6. Macro / Credit Conditions (UMI)
    Upstart's Upstart Macro Index (UMI = 1.39 as of Dec 31, 2025) is the company's proprietary real-time macro calibration metric. A UMI of 1.39 means macro adds ~39% incremental credit risk vs. baseline. When UMI rises, the model tightens automatically — conversion falls, volume misses, stock gets punished. A declining UMI (improving credit conditions) is the largest potential bull catalyst; CEO Girouard noted that improving repayment speeds and easing consumption growth could signal "a long-anticipated tailwind." Rate cuts from the Fed are also unambiguously positive: lower consumer borrowing costs, improved conversion, lower investor return requirements.
