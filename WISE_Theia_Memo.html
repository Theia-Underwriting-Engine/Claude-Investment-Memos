Wise Plc | Preliminary Memo for Theia
1. Business Overview

Wise Plc (WISE-GB) is a global technology company built to make international money movement fast, cheap, convenient, and transparent. Founded in 2011 in London by Kristo Käärmann and Taavet Hinrikus — two Estonians frustrated by bank FX fees — Wise operates what management calls the world's fastest, most efficient cross-border payments infrastructure. The company serves four product lines: (1) Wise Transfers — the original send-money-abroad product, now generating £845B+ in annualized volumes at a take rate of 52 bps vs. banks typically charging 3–5%; (2) Wise Account — a multi-currency account holding £25B+ in customer funds across savings (interest-bearing) and card spend (£15B+ per half-year), with 41% of underlying income now attributable to non-cross-border revenues; (3) Wise Platform (B2B) — a white-label API enabling banks and fintechs to offer Wise's infrastructure to their own customers, accounting for 5% of volumes with a clear path to 10% medium-term and 50% long-term; and (4) Wise Assets — money market fund investing through Wise's own regulated product. Revenue is generated through three mechanisms: cross-border take rate (52 bps avg H1 FY26, continuously declining as efficiencies are passed to customers), card interchange and subscription fees, and the first 1% of interest yield on £25B+ in customer holdings. Wise is LSE-listed (direct listing July 2021) and has announced a dual primary listing in the US in June 2025, targeting a US listing within ~1 year. As of H1 FY26: 13M active customers (+21% YoY), £85B cross-border volume (+24%), 74% of payments arriving in under 20 seconds, NPS 69, 70% of customers acquired via word-of-mouth, and investment-grade BBB credit ratings from both S&P and Fitch.

3. Bull & Bear Cases
▲ Bull Cases
1. Infrastructure Moat Compounds Into Trillion-Dollar Market

Wise's direct integrations into 40+ domestic payment networks create an infrastructure layer that is simultaneously faster (74% instant payments), cheaper (52 bps vs 3–5% bank standard), and more reliable than any competitor. Each new integration (Pix in Brazil, Zengin in Japan, Faster Payments UK) lowers the marginal cost of adding the next route, creating a compounding flywheel: cheaper → more volume → more revenue → more infrastructure investment. The £32 trillion global cross-border TAM is structurally underpenetrated; Wise has ~5% share of the £3T individual segment and far less of the £14T SMB segment.

2. Wise Platform Is a Multi-Billion Revenue Opportunity

With 20,000+ banks globally struggling with outdated correspondent banking infrastructure, Wise Platform offers a ready-made API solution. Management targets Platform reaching 10% of volumes medium-term and 50% long-term. At 50%, with £500B+ in annual volumes at even 40 bps revenue share, Platform could generate £2B+ annually from today's ~£100M. Recent wins — UniCredit, Raiffeisen Bank, Itaú (largest bank in LatAm by assets), Standard Chartered — validate the product at the highest tier of global banking.

3. US Dual Listing Unlocks Institutional Demand and Brand Premium

The proposed US primary listing (announced June 2025, targeting completion ~mid-2026) will expand Wise's investor base to the largest pool of institutional capital globally, provide index inclusion optionality (Russell, S&P international), and dramatically raise brand awareness among US consumers and banks. The US is one of Wise's fastest-growing consumer markets, and the OCC trust charter application signals the company is building US regulatory infrastructure to match. At scale, the US could represent 30%+ of Wise's volumes.

4. Take Rate Stabilization Creates Revenue Re-Acceleration

After a decisive ~15% fee reduction in FY25, Wise's take rate has stabilized at ~52 bps (H1 FY26). As the difficult year-over-year comparison base fades in H2 FY26 and FY27, revenue growth will re-accelerate toward 20%+. CFO Emmanuel Thomassin explicitly noted on the H1 FY26 call: "the real growth will be in H2 when this pricing adjustment is not affecting the comparison." Simultaneously, mix shift toward higher-margin non-cross-border revenue (card, interest, Platform) raises reported blended margins even as cross-border take rate drifts lower.

5. Customer Holdings as a Durable Recurring Revenue Engine

£25B+ in customer holdings at an average 1% yield generates ~£250M in annual interest income at near-zero funding cost. Wise Assets (money market fund) grew AUM 55% YoY in FY25 and is available in Europe, UK, Singapore, and Australia. As interest rates normalize and Holdings grow at 34% CAGR, this becomes an increasingly meaningful, largely uncorrelated recurring revenue stream — a genuine deposit-banking alternative without a banking license risk profile.

▼ Bear Cases
1. Take Rate Structural Erosion Faster Than Expected

Wise's competitive strategy is to continuously reduce fees as it engineers costs out of the platform. At 52 bps today, each 5 bps reduction in take rate on £200B volume represents ~£1B/year in foregone revenue (~62% of FY26E underlying PBT). If Wise reaches 35–40 bps by FY30 (consistent with its infrastructure trajectory), the business requires 30%+ annual volume growth just to hold revenues flat — a tall order as the market matures.

2. Wise Platform Ramps More Slowly Than Anticipated

The B2B Platform is the pivot of the long-term investment thesis, but integration timelines with large financial institutions are notoriously long. Banks typically start with one route or corridor, then expand over 3–5 years. UniCredit, Raiffeisen, and Itaú were announced but volumes are still ramping. If Platform reaches 8% of volumes by FY28 rather than 10–12%, revenue growth disappointments could re-rate the stock materially.

3. US Transaction Tax and Regulatory Headwinds

The proposed US transaction tax on international remittances, if enacted, could structurally suppress US consumer demand for cross-border transfers. Wise operates in 160+ countries and is subject to regulatory risk in every jurisdiction. The dual listing introduces SEC disclosure and US GAAP conversion costs (~£35M one-off in FY26 plus ongoing incremental G&A).

4. Neobank Competition Intensifies as Wise Account Expands

As Wise deepens the Wise Account, it increasingly competes with Revolut (35M customers globally, pursuing banking licenses in UK/EU/US), N26, Monzo, and regional challengers. Revolut's scale (3x Wise's customer base) and banking license pursuit could compress Wise's customer lifetime value. If customers migrate daily banking to Revolut and use Wise only for one-off transfers, the Account vision erodes materially.

5. Cost Structure Bloat from Dual Listing + Hiring Acceleration

In H1 FY26, Wise added 1,000 employees (planned 700 for the full year), invested £57M in marketing (+59%), and guided to £1B+ in total admin expense for FY26 vs. £769M in FY25 — a 30%+ increase. Combined with ~£35M in one-off dual listing costs, reported PBT will likely decline YoY in FY26 despite growing revenue. If the investment phase extends into FY27 with marginal returns lower than expected, the market could reprice Wise at a material discount to current levels.

4. Helmer's 7 Powers
1. Scale Economies STRONG

Wise's cost structure is heavily fixed. Its payments infrastructure, compliance licences, and software platform serve £85B in half-year volume at roughly the same fixed base as it served £40B four years ago. Banking & customer-related fees (COGS) grew only 4% in FY25 vs. 16% underlying income growth — expanding gross margin to 75%. Each incremental £1B in cross-border volume generates ~£5.2M in revenue at near-zero marginal cost (52 bps take rate). This scale economy is the primary reason Wise can offer 52 bps fees while banks charging 300+ bps remain structurally unable to match at equivalent speed and reliability. Competitors at smaller scale (Remitly, OFX) face the same infrastructure investment burden but spread it over smaller volumes, limiting their ability to invest in pricing and product.

2. Network Effects WEAK-MODERATE

Classical multi-sided network effects don't apply (Sender A's experience isn't improved by Sender B being on Wise). What exists is a powerful referral flywheel: ~70% of customers acquired through word-of-mouth, with referred customers arriving at higher average balances and faster multi-product adoption. Wise-to-Wise transfers (both parties on Wise = near-zero cost) create mild network value for recipients. The Platform business creates B2B network value: as more banks join, the routing network becomes richer and route coverage improves for all users. These effects are real but limited in defensibility vs. the strongest network businesses.

3. Counter-Positioning STRONG

Wise's fee structure — 52 bps cross-border vs. 250–500 bps for traditional banks — is structurally impossible for banks to match without destroying their core FX revenue business. Banks generate enormous fee income from cross-border FX markups; Wise's transparent pricing model would cannibalize that revenue if banks adopted it. This was precisely the dynamic that led UBS to abort its £1.4B Wise acquisition in 2022. Wise Platform is the strategic resolution: banks can offer Wise's infrastructure to their customers without destroying their own FX revenue, letting Wise take the pricing pain while the bank retains the customer relationship.

4. Switching Costs MODERATE

Individuals switching from Wise is relatively easy — no long-term contracts, no embedded data lock-in. However, behavioral stickiness is real: users who have set up beneficiaries, enabled auto-conversion, and organized multi-currency accounts face genuine re-enrollment friction. Business customers using Wise for payroll, invoicing (Quickpay), or supply chain payments have deeper integration switching costs. Wise Platform clients (banks) face the deepest switching costs: API integrations, compliance sign-offs, staff training, and route coverage all create multi-year lock-in. Wise explicitly starts Platform clients on one route and expands — each additional route deepens lock-in at minimal incremental cost.

5. Branding MODERATE

Wise has exceptional brand NPS (69 in H1 FY26) and cult-like word-of-mouth among international users. The brand conveys: transparent, cheap, fast, trustworthy — qualities that traditional banks have systematically failed to deliver on cross-border transfers. The 70% word-of-mouth acquisition rate is extraordinary for a financial product and reflects genuine brand advocacy. However, aided awareness outside the expat/immigrant community and tech-savvy demographic remains limited. The US market is the next major brand-building frontier; TV campaigns launched in FY26 are early-stage.

6. Cornered Resource WEAK

Wise doesn't control a scarce non-replicable resource. Its advantage comes from assembled resources: 40+ direct payment network integrations (replicable but time-consuming and expensive), 160+ country regulatory licences (significant time and capital barrier but not permanent), and a talent pool of payments infrastructure engineers. Kristo Käärmann's founder-CEO positioning and deep relationships with regulators globally add intangible value. The proprietary payments routing algorithm is competitive but not patent-protected.

7. Process Power STRONG

Wise's most underappreciated moat. The company has built a culture of deliberate infrastructure investment over shortcut, with a stated philosophy of "avoiding gimmicks" (Käärmann, Q4 2025 earnings). The results: 74% instant payment rates vs. industry average sub-10% for cross-border; 52 bps vs. 250–500 bps incumbents; 75% gross margin on a high-volume regulated financial product. The continuous cycle of engineering cost efficiency, passing savings to customers, then reinvesting resulting volume growth back into infrastructure is a deeply embedded organizational practice — evidenced by (a) 4% COGS growth vs. 16% revenue growth in FY25; (b) 74% instant payments in H1 FY26 vs. 65% at FY25 year-end; (c) NPS 69 sustained while growing customers 21% annually.

5. Porter's 5 Forces
1. Threat of New Entrants MODERATE

Building a global cross-border payments infrastructure is technically feasible but operationally formidable. Obtaining regulatory licences in 160+ countries takes years; direct integrations into domestic payment networks require bilateral relationships and significant technical investment; and customer trust in a money-movement business is not easily bought. The capital requirements are significant (Wise invested ~£3B in its first 10 years). However, big tech (Apple, Google) has distribution but has consistently shied away from regulated financial services due to liability; and well-funded startups with infrastructure talent can accelerate timelines. The OCC trust charter application suggests Wise sees regulatory positioning as a competitive differentiator.

2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers LOW

Wise's primary suppliers are: (a) domestic payment networks and banking partners (for fund custody and settlement), (b) cloud/tech infrastructure (AWS-equivalent), and (c) compliance/KYC data vendors. Banking partners compete actively for Wise's deposit flows; switching between banking partners is operationally achievable. Cloud infrastructure is commodity. Wise's own direct network integrations — connecting directly to national payment systems rather than routing through correspondent banks — reduce dependence on any single supplier and lower transaction costs. This is the core strategic logic of the direct integration program.

3. Bargaining Power of Buyers MODERATE

Individual customers have no direct pricing power. However, competitive pressure from Remitly, Revolut, banks, and other fintechs creates aggregate market discipline — Wise's fee level is constrained by the threat of customer substitution. Business customers have slightly more leverage (higher volume, explicit contracts). Wise Platform clients (banks) negotiate take rates as part of API contract negotiations; larger banks (Itaú, UniCredit) have material leverage given volume commitments. Overall, buyer power is exercised structurally through competitive market pricing rather than individual negotiation.

4. Threat of Substitutes HIGH

This is the most material competitive force. Substitutes include: (a) bank wires (300–500 bps but instantly familiar); (b) Remitly (digital, focused on emerging-market corridors, #2 global digital remittance by volume); (c) PayPal/Xoom (huge distribution); (d) Revolut (multi-currency account with competitive rates); (e) for large transactions, OTC FX providers. For Wise Account, substitutes include high-yield savings at any digital bank and money market funds. The stablecoin ecosystem is an emerging potential substitute — Wise's CEO acknowledged this while positioning Wise as best-in-class for on/off ramps. The high threat of substitutes is the primary reason Wise is committed to continuously lowering fees.

5. Competitive Rivalry MODERATE-HIGH

Direct rivalry is intense in digital remittances (Remitly growing at 30%+ revenue YoY, targeting similar corridors), but Wise maintains differentiation on speed (74% instant), breadth (160+ countries vs. Remitly's narrower emerging-market focus), and the multi-product Account. Western Union and MoneyGram compete on retail cash distribution channels irrelevant to Wise's digital-native positioning. Revolut is the most similar competitor in terms of target demographic — but Revolut is focused on becoming a bank (lending, crypto) while Wise is focused on becoming payments infrastructure. Wise Platform has no direct head-on competitor today; the closest analogues are Swift gpi and Mastercard Cross-Border Services, which are slower and more expensive.

6. What Drives the Stock?
1. Cross-Border Take Rate × Volume = The Core Revenue Equation

Every basis point change in the cross-border take rate on Wise's £175B+ annualized volume (~£9.1M per bps per year in revenue impact) is the single most important stock driver. The 15% fee reduction in FY25 was a deliberate, permanent investment — and the market needed 12–18 months to see the volume response (24% volume growth H1 FY26). Going forward, the debate is: does the take rate stabilize at ~50–52 bps, or does Wise continue cutting toward 40 bps? Bullish: management signals stability at current levels. Bearish: competitive dynamics force further cuts that outpace volume gains.

2. Wise Platform Trajectory — The Multi-Year Re-Rating Catalyst

Platform revenue currently represents ~5% of volumes and ~7% of underlying income. The path from 5% to 10% (medium term) to 50% (long-term) volumes on Platform is the most asymmetric driver of Wise's intrinsic value. Each major bank announcement (UniCredit, Raiffeisen, Itaú, Standard Chartered) is a visible milestone but the real value is in ramp-up: banks start with one corridor and expand over years. The H1 FY26 call confirmed Platform volumes grew 3x faster than overall volumes. If Platform reaches 15% of a growing volume base by FY28, it materially re-rates the stock.

3. US Dual Listing — Institutional Access and Brand Premium

The proposed US primary listing (announced June 2025, targeting ~mid-2026) will be a significant catalyst: (a) index inclusion in Russell/S&P international indices = forced passive buying; (b) expanded US sell-side coverage; (c) US brand marketing tied to the listing creates consumer awareness at scale; (d) higher US liquidity and investor base could compress risk discount on UK-listed shares. OCC trust charter application is a regulatory positioning move that Wall Street will read as bullish for the US long-term story.

4. Customer Holdings and Interest Income Durability

£25B+ in customer holdings at a blended 1% retained yield = ~£250M annual interest income embedded in underlying results. As rates normalize downward, this contribution declines — but Wise offsets this by growing the holdings base (34% CAGR) and expanding Wise Assets (money market fund earning ~4.5% with 20% revenue share). The tension between "rate tailwinds compressing" and "holdings base growing" is a key watch variable in each earnings report.

5. Expense Trajectory and Underlying PBT Margin

FY26 is explicitly an investment year: total admin expense guided to ~£1B (vs £769M in FY25, a 30% increase), absorbing higher headcount, marketing ramp (+59% H1 YoY), and dual listing costs. Management's guidance for underlying PBT margin of "around 16%" suggests discipline within the investment cycle. If expenses come in higher than guided or marketing ROI disappoints (payback period extends beyond 6 months), margins could print below guidance.

6. H2 FY26 Revenue Re-Acceleration

CFO explicitly flagged H2 FY26 as the inflection point where take-rate comparison headwinds ease. If H2 FY26 underlying income grows 20%+ YoY (vs H1's 16%), this provides concrete evidence that the revenue deceleration of the past 12 months was price-cut-driven transient, not structural volume degradation. H2 FY26 results (reported ~June 2026 = FY26 full year results) are therefore the most important near-term catalyst for the stock.

7. What Drives the Business?

Cross-border revenue is a straightforward formula: £ Cross-Border Volume × Take Rate (bps / 10,000). H1 FY26 actual: £85B × 52 bps = £442M in H1 (£884M annualized). Volume is driven by active customers × avg transactions × avg transaction size. The take rate is driven by Wise's infrastructure cost pass-through policy — the company commits to reducing fees as efficiencies compound, making the take rate a function of the technology roadmap, not pricing strategy.

Non-cross-border revenue diversifies the model meaningfully: card revenue is card spend volume × interchange rate (~0.87% blended), generating ~£132M in H1 FY26 on £15B+ card spend. Interest income is customer holdings × 1% retained yield = ~£250M annually on £25B holdings. Platform revenue is platform volumes × revenue share (~20–25 bps) — currently ~5% of £170B total volume = £8.5B × ~22 bps = ~£187M annualized and growing at 3x total volume pace.

8. AlphaSense Insights

Note: AlphaSense Deep Research Report dated March 10, 2026 (PDF encrypted/unavailable). Key themes synthesized from H1 FY26 and Q4 FY25 earnings call discussions, sell-side commentary, and publicly available research.

Theme 1 — Take Rate Floor Debate: Where Does the Price Moat Stabilize?

Multiple sell-side analysts (Morgan Stanley's Adam Wood, UBS's Justin Forsythe) pressed management on the take rate trajectory. Consensus view: the decisive 15% cut in FY25 was deliberate moat-deepening (Kristo acknowledged "we see this working, we see more volume even coming up in the short term") and take rate should stabilize at 50–52 bps for the next 12–18 months before resuming gradual decline. Dissenting bear view (Citi/BofA): take rate has further to fall as Platform volumes (which carry lower take rates) grow as a share of mix, further compressing the blended rate even if core consumer take rate stabilizes.

Theme 2 — Wise Platform: When Does 10% Volume Target Become Visible?

Platform at 5% of volumes (1% increase from April 2025) growing ~3x total volume pace. Management identified volumes ramping on "older" integrations (Standard Chartered, Google Pay, etc.) as the primary driver — not just new name announcements. Analyst focus: building a more transparent reporting cadence on Platform revenue vs. volume. Barclays (Grégoire Hermann) pushed for Raiffeisen timeline; management declined specific guidance but noted they "announce when we're starting to see some of their volumes." Bull thesis on Platform is 2–3 year, not 12-month. Key watch: first earnings report showing Platform at 6%+ of volumes = meaningful confirmation.

Theme 3 — US Listing as Multi-Year Strategic Inflection

Announced at Q4 FY25 earnings (June 2025). Timeline: shareholder circular end-June 2025, AGM vote end-July 2025, SEC filing ~1 year. Cantor Fitzgerald (Bharath Nagaraj) and BofA pressed on listing requirements (US GAAP conversion, reincorporation, management presence). Management confirmed: not moving HQ, retaining UK listing, US GAAP transition underway. Institutional thesis: access to 4,000 US banks for Platform, brand lift in largest remittance market, and potential S&P index inclusion. Near-term: OCC trust charter application is a regulatory positioning move Wall Street reads as bullish for the US long-term story.

Theme 4 — Stablecoin: Threat or Opportunity?

Most debated strategic question across both earnings calls. CEO Käärmann's view: Wise's infrastructure (fast, regulated, local currency on/off ramps) is exactly what stablecoin use cases require; the hard part is regulated connectivity to local markets, which is Wise's core competency. Expert debate: will stablecoin-native players (Bridge, BVNK) build competing local payment network connectivity, or is Wise's regulatory moat (licences in 20+ countries) genuinely non-replicable within 5 years? Current Theia take: Wise benefits from stablecoin growth as the best-positioned fiat on/off ramp; threat only materializes if stablecoin players spend 5–10 years replicating Wise's licence stack.

Theme 5 — Margin Guidance Credibility Post-FY26 Investment Cycle

H1 FY26 saw underlying PBT margin at "top of range" (~16–17% ex listing costs) despite a 30% admin expense ramp. Management credibility from CFO Emmanuel Thomassin's disciplined framing ("the money we win, we don't spend, we invest, we want to have a return"). H2 FY26 margin expected to compress (more investment, higher H2 listing costs) before normalizing in FY27. Street is pricing in ~15–16% normalized margin. Key risk: if marketing spend in US/Canada/New Zealand shows payback extending beyond 6 months, the market will question whether the expense ramp is truly disciplined. Marketing payback period disclosure (currently "within 6 months" guideline) is the most important margin-related data point to track.
