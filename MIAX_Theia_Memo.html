Miami International Holdings | Preliminary Memo for Theia
1. Business Overview

Miami International Holdings, Inc. (NYSE: MIAX) is a technology-driven operator of regulated financial marketplaces across multiple asset classes and geographies. Founded in 2007 by a team with extensive trading and technology experience, MIAX launched its first options exchange in December 2012 with a mission to build a differentiated exchange model focused on customer-centricity, competitive pricing, and outstanding technology. The company has grown from a single options exchange into the 13th largest global derivatives exchange operator following a successful $397 million IPO in August 2025.

MIAX operates four distinct business segments: Options (81.2% of FY2025 revenue), which encompasses four U.S. national securities exchanges (MIAX Options, MIAX Pearl, MIAX Emerald, MIAX Sapphire) that achieved a record 17.1% annual market share of the U.S. multi-listed options market in 2025; Equities (11.3% of revenue), operating MIAX Pearl Equities for cash equity trading; Futures (6.4% of revenue), anchored by MIAX Futures (formerly Minneapolis Grain Exchange) with an exclusive 10-year Bloomberg licensing agreement for equity index derivatives launching Q2 2026; and International (1.1% of revenue), including the Bermuda Stock Exchange and the newly acquired International Stock Exchange (TISE) in Guernsey. The company's proprietary, in-house-built technology platform featuring deterministic architecture and 99.9998% operational uptime since 2012 serves as its primary competitive moat, enabling sub-200 microsecond latency that outpaces Cboe (250-400μs) and NYSE (400-700μs).

The company's sub-200 microsecond latency advantage continues to attract HFT market makers. Fortress Balance Sheet. Post-IPO, MIAX ended 2025 with $434M cash and virtually no debt ($1.5M). 4. Helmer's 7 Powers

1. Scale Economies Strong
MIAX's exchange infrastructure has enormous fixed costs — the proprietary technology platform, SEC regulatory compliance apparatus, co-location data center facilities, 24/7 operations monitoring, and redundant disaster recovery systems — while facing near-zero marginal cost per additional contract traded. This creates massive operating leverage that is now visibly manifesting in financials: adjusted EBITDA margins expanded from 30% in FY2024 to 46% in FY2025, with Q4 2025 reaching 50%. Once the technology stack is built and regulatory approval secured, each incremental contract processed falls almost entirely to gross profit. The same matching engine that processes 5 million contracts per day can process 10 million with negligible incremental cost — only additional network capacity and marginal regulatory reporting burden. The scale moat is substantial: new entrants face $100M+ initial investment just to meet minimum viable product requirements for an options exchange, plus 2-3 years of SEC approval process, plus the bootstrapping challenge of attracting initial liquidity without an established customer base. MIAX has successfully scaled from startup to the 13th largest global derivatives exchange operator, and each incremental market share point now delivers outsized margin expansion.

2. Network Effects Moderate
Exchange network effects operate through the liquidity flywheel: more market makers quoting on MIAX create tighter bid-ask spreads, which attracts more retail and institutional order flow seeking best execution, which in turn attracts more market makers seeking counterparty flow. MIAX successfully bootstrapped this flywheel over 2012-2025 using a combination of its technology advantage (sub-200μs latency attracting HFT market makers first) and Equity Rights Programs that granted warrants to market makers meeting volume thresholds. The result is a self-reinforcing ecosystem where MIAX's top market makers — including Citadel Securities, Susquehanna, and Wolverine — have become deeply embedded in the platform. However, the network effect is moderated by the structural reality of multi-listed options: unlike proprietary products that can only trade on one venue (Cboe's VIX, CME's E-minis), multi-listed equity options can legally trade on any of 16 U.S. options exchanges. Order routing systems continuously arbitrage price differentials across venues, meaning liquidity can migrate if a competitor offers superior execution or lower fees. The network effect is real and provides meaningful protection — but it is interruptible, not permanent. A competitor achieving technological parity could gradually erode MIAX's liquidity advantage over 3-5 years.

3. Counter-Positioning Strong
MIAX's core counter-positioning is that legacy exchanges — Cboe, NYSE, Nasdaq — cannot replicate its sub-200 microsecond deterministic latency without undertaking a multi-year, operationally risky overhaul of their legacy technology infrastructure. These incumbents built their matching engines in the 1990s and 2000s on architectures optimized for reliability and regulatory compliance, not HFT-grade microsecond performance. Retrofitting this infrastructure requires massive capital investment ($50-100M+), years of development, and carries severe operational risk: any glitch during migration could cause trading halts, regulatory scrutiny, and reputational damage. CME's failed attempt to modernize via a Google Cloud partnership — ultimately abandoned after years of effort — illustrates this constraint vividly. MIAX, by contrast, built its entire technology stack from scratch in 2007-2012 specifically for HFT requirements: deterministic architecture where every message is processed in guaranteed time, 99.9998% uptime since launch, and the new Onyx futures platform achieving sub-10 microsecond latency (vs. CME's ~50μs). Incumbent CEOs face a classic innovator's dilemma: investing $100M+ to match MIAX's latency would cannibalize current high-margin businesses while carrying execution risk, so they rationally choose incremental improvements instead. This counter-positioning is genuine, durable, and likely to persist for 5+ years.

4. Switching Costs Moderate
Market makers who have invested heavily in optimizing for MIAX's platform face real switching costs across multiple dimensions. Technical switching costs include: re-engineering latency-sensitive routing algorithms calibrated to MIAX's specific order matching logic; reconfiguring co-location infrastructure (servers physically housed in MIAX's data center); rewriting connectivity software that interfaces with MIAX's proprietary API specifications; and rebuilding statistical models trained on MIAX-specific market microstructure data. Operational switching costs include: compliance onboarding with new exchange partners (legal, regulatory, and KYC processes); staff retraining on new platform interfaces; and establishing new vendor relationships for connectivity services. For a sophisticated HFT firm, optimizing a new venue can take 6-12 months and $1-2M in engineering resources. However, the switching cost moat is moderated by multi-homing: most large electronic market makers (Citadel, Susquehanna, Jane Street) trade simultaneously across all 16 U.S. options exchanges, maintaining optimized infrastructure at each venue. They can shift volume allocation at the margin without fully "switching" — making MIAX's protection partial rather than absolute. Switching costs provide meaningful friction but don't create lock-in.

5. Branding Weak
MIAX has developed strong brand recognition among electronic market makers, HFT firms, and institutional traders who interact directly with exchange technology — but exchange "brand" operates fundamentally differently than consumer branding and doesn't command pricing power in the traditional sense. Unlike consumer products where brand loyalty drives habitual purchasing regardless of price, exchange trading routes to wherever offers best execution, tightest spreads, and lowest fees, regardless of the exchange operator's brand perception. An order router doesn't have brand loyalty; it has algorithms optimized for execution quality. MIAX has successfully cultivated a reputation as the technology-forward challenger to legacy incumbents — the "fast, modern, customer-focused" alternative to established players — and this positioning has attracted market makers who value these attributes. CEO Thomas Gallagher's personal reputation (named Chief Executive of the Year at the 2026 FOW International Awards) adds credibility. However, this is functional positioning rather than durable brand equity: if a competitor matched MIAX's technology and pricing, the "innovative challenger" positioning would lose its differentiation. Brand provides marginal benefit in attracting talent, institutional capital, and partnership opportunities, but is not a source of sustainable competitive advantage.

6. Cornered Resource Moderate
MIAX controls several cornered resources that are difficult for competitors to replicate. First, and most tangibly, MIAX holds SEC licenses to operate four national securities exchanges (MIAX Options, MIAX Pearl, MIAX Emerald, MIAX Sapphire) — the approval process takes 2-3 years of intensive regulatory review, requires demonstrating robust surveillance and compliance capabilities, and involves ongoing examination requirements that deter casual entry. Second, the proprietary technology stack built over 15+ years embodies accumulated engineering knowledge that would take years and $100M+ to replicate: deterministic matching architecture, 99.9998% uptime history since 2012, and deep optimization for HFT requirements. This isn't just hardware — it's the institutional knowledge of how to build and operate ultra-low-latency infrastructure. Third, the exclusive 10-year Bloomberg licensing agreement for equity index futures (B500, B100Q indices) is a genuinely scarce resource: Bloomberg chose MIAX as their exclusive partner over established incumbents like CME and Cboe, creating a proprietary product opportunity unavailable to competitors. Fourth, MIAX's 15-year track record of clean regulatory compliance and zero significant enforcement actions is a reputational asset that de-risks new product launches. However, these resources are meaningful but not absolutely unique — other exchanges hold similar licenses and could theoretically build comparable technology given sufficient capital and time.

7. Process Power Strong
MIAX has developed genuine process capabilities in high-performance exchange technology that are difficult for competitors to replicate even with unlimited capital. The engineering culture that produced sub-10 microsecond latency on the Onyx futures platform — approximately 5x faster than CME's matching engine — reflects institutional knowledge accumulated over 15 years of iterative optimization. This isn't just code that can be hired away with a few key engineers; it's embedded organizational knowledge about hardware selection, network topology, software architecture, latency measurement, and operational procedures that exists across hundreds of employees and is encoded in processes, documentation, and tooling. CEO Thomas Gallagher's leadership team has been with the company since founding in 2007 — CIO Douglas Schafer, General Counsel Barbara Comly, and other key executives — providing management continuity that supports consistent execution and institutional memory. The "customer-first" culture, documented in SEC filings and validated in AlphaSense expert calls, manifests in MIAX's willingness to tailor exchange features to specific HFT requirements: custom order types, allocation model modifications, and connectivity options developed through direct collaboration with market makers. This responsiveness — essentially treating institutional customers as development partners rather than passive users — creates sticky relationships that competitors with more bureaucratic cultures struggle to replicate.

Summary: The strongest moats are Scale Economies, Counter-Positioning against legacy incumbents, and Process Power in exchange technology. These represent genuine structural advantages that should persist over the medium term. Network effects and switching costs provide additional but more modest protection.

5. Porter's 5 Forces

1. Threat of New Entrants Low
Barriers to entry in U.S. options exchanges are exceptionally high, representing one of the most protected competitive environments in financial services. Requirements include: (a) SEC approval for a national securities exchange license — a multi-year process requiring extensive legal filings, demonstrated surveillance capabilities, technology audits, and ongoing regulatory oversight; (b) $100M+ investment in technology infrastructure including matching engines, order routing, market data distribution, and co-location facilities; (c) clearing relationships with the Options Clearing Corporation (OCC), the single clearinghouse for all U.S. listed options; (d) most critically, the ability to attract initial liquidity from skeptical market makers who have no incentive to provide quotes on a new venue with zero order flow. Industry experts note that barriers are significantly higher in options than in equities due to the complexity of options quoting (market makers must quote thousands of strikes and expirations per underlying, requiring sophisticated pricing models and risk management). MIAX was the last successful new entrant in 2012, and no credible new competitor has emerged in 14 years — despite the attractiveness of exchange economics. IEX's 2024 attempt to launch an options exchange was abandoned after years of planning. The threat of new entry is very low.

2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers Low
MIAX's primary "suppliers" are technology vendors (servers, networking equipment, FPGA chips, data center services) and clearing infrastructure (the Options Clearing Corporation). The company has strategically minimized supplier power by building most critical technology in-house: the matching engine, order routing logic, market data systems, and surveillance infrastructure are all proprietary. This vertical integration means MIAX is not dependent on any single external technology vendor for its core competitive capabilities. Hardware procurement (Dell/HP servers, Cisco/Arista networking, Xilinx FPGAs) is diversified across multiple vendors with interchangeable components. OCC is a shared industry utility owned collectively by the exchanges themselves, with standardized pricing and no ability to discriminate against individual members. Data center services (co-location, power, cooling) are available from multiple competing providers. The one area of emerging supply chain risk is AI infrastructure: if tech giants continue procuring vast quantities of high-performance computing hardware for AI training, there could be temporary shortages affecting MIAX's ability to upgrade trading systems. However, MIAX has no single-source dependencies and maintains 12+ month inventory buffers for critical components. Overall supplier power is low and not a meaningful competitive constraint.

3. Bargaining Power of Buyers High
MIAX's customers — primarily electronic market makers and HFT firms like Citadel Securities, Susquehanna, Wolverine, and Jane Street — have substantial bargaining power that constrains MIAX's pricing flexibility. The top three customers represent 41% of revenues (15%, 14%, and 12% respectively), creating meaningful concentration risk: loss of a single major customer could reduce revenues by 10-15% and trigger adverse selection spirals as other market makers perceive reduced liquidity. These sophisticated institutional customers can easily route order flow to any of 16 competing U.S. options exchanges based on fee structures, execution quality, and rebate tiers. They employ dedicated teams analyzing exchange economics and can shift volume allocation within days in response to fee changes. MIAX must compete aggressively on maker rebates (payments to market makers for providing liquidity), taker fees, connectivity pricing, and market data costs — a perpetual treadmill of fee optimization that compresses margins industry-wide. The customer concentration is exacerbated by the structure of HFT: the top 5-10 electronic market makers account for the vast majority of options volume across all exchanges, meaning MIAX has limited ability to diversify its customer base. Buyer power is high and represents a structural constraint on margin expansion in the multi-listed options business.

4. Threat of Substitutes Moderate
The primary substitutes for listed equity options are OTC derivatives (bilateral contracts negotiated between sophisticated counterparties), futures contracts (which can replicate option-like payoffs through dynamic hedging), and synthetic option strategies constructed from other instruments. For most hedging and speculation purposes, listed options on regulated exchanges remain the preferred instrument due to transparency (publicly quoted prices), liquidity (continuous markets with tight spreads), counterparty risk mitigation (OCC central clearing), and regulatory acceptance (pension funds and institutional investors can easily access listed options). The structural advantages of listed options are significant, and the market has grown consistently at 10%+ annually for decades. However, substitute threats are evolving in two dimensions. First, crypto derivatives traded on offshore venues (Deribit, Binance) provide alternative speculation vehicles for retail traders seeking leverage and volatility exposure — though these don't directly compete for traditional equity hedging use cases. Second, zero-days-to-expiration (0DTE) options have cannibalized some activity that previously occurred in weekly and monthly options, shifting volume toward shorter-dated contracts with different exchange economics. Third, the rise of structured products and ETFs that embed options exposure (covered call ETFs, buffer ETFs) may reduce direct options trading by end-investors, though this increases institutional options demand. Overall threat is moderate: listed options have structural advantages that protect market share, but the competitive landscape for derivative exposure is dynamic.

5. Competitive Rivalry High
The U.S. options exchange market is intensely competitive, with 16 registered exchanges operated by four major exchange groups (Cboe: 4 exchanges; Nasdaq: 6 exchanges; NYSE: 4 exchanges; MIAX: 4 exchanges) plus smaller independents. Competition primarily occurs across three vectors: (a) pricing — constant adjustments to maker-taker rebates and fee tiers, with exchanges announcing fee changes monthly and major market makers actively arbitraging pricing across venues; (b) technology — latency, uptime, determinism, and specialized order types, where MIAX's sub-200μs advantage drives market share gains; (c) product features — allocation models (pro-rata vs. price-time priority), order types (complex orders, IOC variants), and market structure innovations. The rivalry is fundamentally zero-sum for multi-listed products: every contract MIAX wins is a contract directly lost by Cboe, Nasdaq, or NYSE, creating a permanent tug-of-war for market share. This dynamic compresses margins industry-wide as exchanges compete for volume through rebates rather than product differentiation. The only escape from zero-sum competition is proprietary products — Cboe's exclusive SPX and VIX options command premium pricing because they can't trade elsewhere, generating 50%+ EBITDA margins versus 20-30% for multi-listed products. MIAX's strategic pivot toward Bloomberg index futures represents an attempt to access this premium economics. Overall competitive intensity is high and will remain so for the foreseeable future in the core multi-listed options business.

Summary: The competitive environment is challenging. High buyer power and intense rivalry create persistent margin pressure on multi-listed products. However, exceptionally low threat of new entry and low supplier power provide structural protection. MIAX's path to premium economics requires successful expansion into proprietary products (Bloomberg index futures).

6. What Drives the Stock?

The following are the key variables that would move MIAX's share price materially in the next 12 months:

1. Options Market Share Trajectory. MIAX achieved 18.2% market share in Q4 2025. Every quarterly update will be scrutinized for continued momentum vs. a ceiling forming at ~20%. Share gains are bullish (operating leverage); share loss or stagnation would trigger de-rating as investors question competitive durability.

2. Bloomberg Index Futures Launch Success. The Q2 2026 launch of B500/B100Q futures is the single most important catalyst for the growth narrative. Success metrics include: (a) market maker adoption; (b) initial volume trajectory; (c) open interest buildup. A successful launch validates MIAX's ability to compete in proprietary products; failure would confirm the bear thesis that MIAX remains stuck in commoditized multi-listed options.

3. U.S. Options Industry Volume. MIAX's revenues are ~60% transaction-based and directly tied to industry-wide options volume. Elevated volatility drives options usage for hedging and speculation. VIX levels, macro uncertainty, and retail trading activity all influence total addressable volume. A volatility collapse would compress industry volumes regardless of market share.

4. Revenue Per Contract (RPC) Trends. Options RPC improved 18.7% in 2025 to $0.108 due to favorable product mix. Analysts will track RPC trajectory as an indicator of pricing power vs. competitive fee pressure. RPC compression would indicate market share gains are coming at the cost of economics.

5. Adjusted EBITDA Margin Progression. Q4 2025 margins reached 50%. Management guides for continued operating leverage as infrastructure investments mature. Margin expansion validates the scale economics thesis; margin compression would signal either competitive pricing pressure or operating cost creep.

6. Capital Allocation Announcements. With $434M cash and negligible debt, MIAX has significant deployment optionality. M&A (accretive exchange acquisitions), buybacks (at current discount to target), or dividend initiation would all be discrete catalysts. Aggressive, dilutive acquisition would be negatively received.
